Step-Up in Basis for California Inherited Property

When someone inherits California real estate, one of the most important tax concepts they need to understand is stepped-up basis. It is widely misunderstood, frequently overlooked, and in many cases worth more than people realize. For a family inheriting a home in California, where values have appreciated dramatically over decades, the step-up in basis can eliminate what would otherwise be a six-figure capital gains tax bill.

This guide explains what stepped-up basis means, how it works, where it gets complicated, and how it interacts with the other tax and legal issues that arise when California real estate changes hands at death.

What Step-Up in Basis Means

Basis is the starting point for calculating capital gains. When you sell an asset, your taxable gain is the difference between what you sold it for and your basis. If you sell a property for $1.2 million and your basis is $200,000, you have a $1 million gain subject to capital gains tax. If your basis is $1.1 million, you have a $100,000 gain.

When property is purchased, the buyer's basis is generally the purchase price. That basis adjusts over time for improvements, depreciation, and certain other events. But it starts at cost.

When property is inherited, the rules change. Under federal tax law, the heir's basis in inherited property is generally reset to the fair market value of the property on the date of the decedent's death. See IRC § 1014; California Revenue and Taxation Code § 17024.5 (California conformity to federal basis rules). This reset is called a stepped-up basis, because in most cases the value at death is higher than what the decedent originally paid, so the basis steps up.

The practical consequence is significant. If a parent bought a California home in 1985 for $180,000 and it is worth $1.4 million at death, the heir inherits it with a basis of $1.4 million, not $180,000. If the heir sells shortly after at $1.4 million, the capital gain is zero. The $1.22 million in appreciation that occurred during the parent's lifetime is never taxed as income to anyone.

How It Works in Practice

The step-up applies to the fair market value of the property at the date of death. That value is typically established by a qualified appraisal. For real estate, this means a formal appraisal by a licensed California appraiser, completed as of the date of death. For estate administration purposes, a probate referee may also establish value.

The stepped-up basis applies regardless of whether the property goes through probate. Property passing through a trust, by operation of law, or through probate all receive the step-up if the property was included in the decedent's taxable estate.

If the heir sells the property above the stepped-up value, they owe capital gains on the difference. If they sell below it, they may have a loss. If they sell at or near the stepped-up value, they owe little or nothing.

Example

Parent purchased a San Jose home in 1990 for $220,000. At the parent's death in 2025, the home is appraised at $1.6 million. The heir's basis is $1.6 million.

If the heir sells six months later for $1.65 million, the taxable gain is $50,000, not $1.43 million. Federal long-term capital gains rates apply to that $50,000.

If the heir sells for $1.6 million, there is no taxable gain at all.

Trusts and Basis

Whether trust-owned property receives a step-up in basis depends on how the trust is structured and whether the property is included in the decedent's taxable estate.

Revocable living trusts. Property held in a revocable living trust at the grantor's death is included in the taxable estate and receives a full step-up in basis. This is the most common situation for California families who have done basic estate planning. The trust avoids probate, and the beneficiaries still get the stepped-up basis.

Irrevocable trusts. Property transferred to an irrevocable trust before death may or may not receive a step-up, depending on the structure. If the property is included in the taxable estate, it receives a step-up. If it was transferred in a way that removes it from the taxable estate, no step-up applies. The tax consequences of irrevocable trust structures are complex and depend on specific trust terms and applicable tax law.

Joint tenancy with right of survivorship. When one joint tenant dies, the surviving joint tenant receives a step-up in basis, but only for the decedent's share. For a property held equally by two joint tenants, the survivor's basis for the inherited half steps up to fair market value, while the survivor's own original half retains its original basis.

Community Property and the Double Step-Up

California is a community property state, which creates an important advantage for married couples. See California Probate Code § 68 (definition of community property); IRC § 1014(b)(6) (basis of community property). When one spouse dies, community property held by the couple receives a step-up in basis on both halves of the property, not just the decedent's half. This is sometimes called the double step-up.

For married couples who own California real estate as community property, this means the surviving spouse's basis in the entire property is reset to fair market value at the date of the first spouse's death. If the surviving spouse then sells the property, the capital gain is calculated from that fully stepped-up basis on both halves.

Why this matters for married California homeowners. A couple that bought a Los Angeles home in 1995 for $350,000 and holds it as community property receives a full step-up on both halves when one spouse dies. If the home is worth $2 million at that point, the surviving spouse's basis becomes $2 million. A sale shortly after at $2 million produces no capital gains tax. Holding the same property as joint tenancy rather than community property would result in a step-up on only the decedent's half, leaving the surviving spouse with a blended basis considerably lower than $2 million.

Married couples who own California real estate should confirm how title is held. If the property is held as joint tenancy, converting it to community property with right of survivorship may be worth considering from a tax planning perspective, though any title change should be reviewed carefully for property tax, lender, and legal implications.

How It Interacts with Proposition 19

Step-up in basis and Proposition 19 address different taxes and operate independently. It is important to understand both and not confuse them.

Step-up in basis is a federal income tax concept. It determines the starting point for calculating capital gains when inherited property is eventually sold. California conforms to the federal rule and does not impose a separate state basis rule or a state estate tax.

Proposition 19 is a California property tax concept. It determines whether the property's assessed value for property tax purposes is reassessed when inherited. Since February 2021, Proposition 19 has significantly limited the parent-child exclusion from reassessment. Most inherited California real estate is now reassessed to current market value when transferred, resulting in higher annual property taxes.

These two rules interact in one important practical way: a family inheriting a California home may avoid all capital gains tax on a sale (because of the stepped-up basis) while simultaneously facing a significant increase in annual property taxes going forward (because of Proposition 19 reassessment). Understanding both is essential to evaluating whether to sell or keep the property.

Sell or Keep: The Basis Question

The stepped-up basis is one of the most important factors in the sell-or-keep decision for inherited California property. But it is not the only one, and it interacts with other considerations in ways that matter.

Selling shortly after inheritance. Selling near the date of death, when the sale price is close to the stepped-up basis, minimizes or eliminates capital gains tax. This is generally the most tax-efficient outcome from an income tax perspective. It also avoids the risk of future depreciation that would create a loss below the stepped-up basis.

Keeping the property. If the heir keeps the property, the stepped-up basis becomes the starting point for future gain. Any appreciation after the date of death will eventually be taxable when sold. The annual property tax under Proposition 19 reassessment adds to the cost of holding. Rental income, if applicable, is taxable. Whether keeping makes financial sense depends on a full analysis of expected appreciation, carrying costs, Proposition 19 impact, and the heir's own tax situation.

Basis for rental or depreciation purposes. If the heir converts the inherited property to a rental, the stepped-up basis is also the starting point for depreciation deductions. This can produce meaningful tax benefits over time but adds complexity to the eventual sale calculation.

Common Mistakes

Not getting an appraisal at the date of death. The stepped-up basis is tied to value at the date of death. If no appraisal is done at that time and the family sells years later, establishing the stepped-up basis becomes more difficult and potentially subject to IRS challenge. Getting a qualified appraisal as of the date of death is a standard and important step in estate administration.

Assuming the step-up applies to all assets. The step-up in basis applies to inherited assets. It does not apply to gifts made during the donor's lifetime. Property given to a family member as a gift during life carries over the donor's original basis, not the fair market value at the time of the gift. This is called carryover basis, and it is a significant difference from inherited property.

Confusing step-up with Proposition 19. As described above, these are separate concepts. The fact that an heir receives a stepped-up basis does not mean the property taxes will be low. The fact that property taxes increased due to Proposition 19 reassessment does not affect the income tax basis calculation.

Failing to account for improvements. The stepped-up basis at the date of death includes the value of improvements made by the decedent. But improvements made by the heir after inheriting the property add to the heir's basis, reducing future gain. Keeping records of post-inheritance improvements is worth doing from the start.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is step-up in basis for inherited property?

When you inherit property, your cost basis for federal income tax purposes is generally reset to the fair market value of the property on the date of the decedent's death. If you sell the property shortly after inheriting it at or near that value, you owe little or no capital gains tax.

Does California have its own step-up in basis rule?

California conforms to the federal step-up in basis rule for inherited property. There is no separate California rule that overrides or limits the federal stepped-up basis. California does not have an estate tax or inheritance tax.

Does a step-up in basis apply to trust property in California?

It depends on the type of trust. Property held in a revocable living trust generally receives a full step-up in basis at the grantor's death. Property transferred to an irrevocable trust before death may not receive a step-up, depending on how the trust is structured and whether the assets are included in the taxable estate.

How does step-up in basis interact with Proposition 19?

They address different taxes and operate independently. A property can receive a full step-up in basis for income tax purposes and still be fully reassessed for property tax purposes under Proposition 19. Understanding both is essential to evaluating the sell-or-keep decision.

What happens to basis if I keep the inherited property instead of selling?

Your basis becomes the stepped-up value at the date of death. If you later sell, your gain is calculated from that stepped-up basis. If the property appreciates further after you inherit it, you will owe capital gains on that additional appreciation when you eventually sell.
