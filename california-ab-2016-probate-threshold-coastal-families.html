California Raised the Probate Threshold to $750,000. Here's Why Most Coastal Families Still Won't Benefit.

By William Plevy · Attorney & California Real Estate Broker (DRE #01956776) · Published July 2026

Every California real estate transaction touching an inherited property runs into probate rules. As an attorney who advises on the transactional side and a broker who sees the sales that follow, I have watched families lose meaningful equity to a system most of them did not know how to navigate.

For decades, the story was the same. Families inheriting a parent's California home faced probate. Even for modest homes with clear title and cooperative heirs, families were forced through a court process that typically ran 12 to 18 months and cost 4 to 7 percent of the estate's gross value in statutory fees.

In 2025, California quietly changed the rules for some of these families. Understanding who benefits, and who is left out, matters more than the headline suggests.

What AB 2016 Changed

California's Assembly Bill 2016, effective April 1, 2025, raised the threshold for a simplified probate procedure from $184,500 to $750,000. The change applies only to a decedent's primary residence.

The old threshold had been effectively useless for years. When it was set, California's median home values were far lower. By 2020, the $184,500 limit excluded nearly every California homeowner. AB 2016 was long overdue.

Under the new law, families can use a Petition to Determine Succession to Primary Residence, filed under Probate Code Sections 13150-13158 using Judicial Council Form DE-310, to transfer title to a qualifying primary residence without opening formal probate.

Who Qualifies

Four requirements must all be met.

Primary residence only. The property must have been the decedent's primary residence at the time of death. Investment properties, rental homes, vacation homes, and undeveloped land do not qualify.

Gross value under $750,000. California measures against fair market value at date of death, not net equity after mortgage. A home worth $800,000 with a $200,000 mortgage does not qualify, even though family equity is only $600,000.

Death on or after April 1, 2025. Deaths before that date fall under the old $184,500 threshold and require traditional probate.

Forty-day waiting period. At least forty days must pass after the date of death before filing.

For families who qualify, the numbers are meaningful. A qualifying petition typically resolves faster than the 12 to 24 months of full probate, often within a few months. Full probate on comparable estates can result in statutory probate fees reaching tens of thousands of dollars, calculated under California Probate Code Section 10810. That is real relief for real families. The Central Valley, Inland Empire, and older neighborhoods in less-appreciated markets contain thousands of California homes in the $500,000 to $750,000 range.

The Coastal California Reality

The problem is that most California housing wealth sits in places where AB 2016 does not apply.

According to the California Association of Realtors, the statewide median home price reached a record $930,260 in May 2026, well above the AB 2016 threshold. Eleven California counties had median home prices above $1 million as of early 2026: Alameda, Marin, Mono, Napa, Orange, San Diego, San Francisco, San Mateo, Santa Barbara, Santa Clara, and Santa Cruz. San Mateo County alone reported a median above $1.5 million.

For families inheriting property in any of these markets, the $750,000 threshold provides no relief. Full probate remains the required path unless the property was held in a trust before death.

The Gap the Law Left Open

Consider a common scenario. A family inherits a Bay Area home worth $1.2 million, held in the parent's individual name without a trust.

Under full probate, this family faces court process of 12 to 18 months. Statutory attorney fees calculated under Probate Code Section 10810 would total approximately $25,000, with a matching amount available to the executor, plus court filing fees, probate referee fees, and other administrative costs. Court confirmation for the sale can also reduce flexibility, lengthen the sales process, and limit the pool of potential buyers compared with a standard residential listing.

The exact same family, if the property had been held in a properly funded trust, would generally avoid probate entirely. Trust administration is often substantially less expensive and faster than formal probate, although costs vary depending on the assets, complexity of administration, and any disputes.

The difference between these outcomes is often the difference between preserving generational wealth and losing a meaningful percentage of it. AB 2016 did not close this gap. For most coastal California families, it made the gap more visible.

What Both Groups Should Actually Do

For families with qualifying properties in the $500,000 to $750,000 range. The simplified petition may genuinely help. Talk to a California estate attorney about whether the property qualifies. Verify the primary residence question, get an appraisal early to confirm the value falls below $750,000, and understand that the forty-day waiting period cannot be shortened. The California Courts Self-Help center provides guidance on Form DE-310 procedures.

For families with California homes above $750,000. The urgency of trust planning just became clearer. AB 2016 explicitly declined to protect properties above that threshold. The families in the $750,000 to $2 million range without trust planning are the largest population of California families whose inheritance gets meaningfully eroded by probate cost and time.

For families already in probate with a property above $750,000. Ask specifically about Independent Administration of Estates Act authority, governed by Probate Code Sections 10400-10592. Where the court grants IAEA authority, executors can complete many actions, including selling real property, through a Notice of Proposed Action (Form DE-165) with fifteen days advance notice rather than court confirmation. IAEA authority is requested when filing the Petition for Probate but is not automatic and can be limited or denied by the court.

Common Pitfalls

Several patterns catch families off guard, whether they qualify for the simplified petition or not.

Watch for these issues

  • Probate and Proposition 19 are entirely separate. Probate determines how title transfers after death. Proposition 19 determines how the property will be taxed after that transfer. Neither the simplified petition nor IAEA authority affects Prop 19 reassessment. If heirs take title through any procedure and do not make the home their primary residence within twelve months, the property gets reassessed to current market value.
  • The gross value trap. For the simplified petition specifically, the $750,000 threshold measures the property's fair market value at date of death, not equity. Borderline cases require careful appraisal.
  • Title company scrutiny. Some California title companies have been slow to fully accept the simplified petition orders. Working with a title company experienced with succession petitions matters.

The Broader Lesson

The clearest lesson from AB 2016 is what it did not do. California's legislature extended simplified administration to a broader group of families than before, but stopped short of the median coastal California home value.

That decision was probably right. Truly large estates should have court oversight. Modest estates should not be crushed by fees. But the line was drawn in a place that leaves most of California's actual inherited property decisions untouched.

The families in the middle, holding homes worth $750,000 to $2 million without trust planning, remain the largest population of California families whose inheritance is meaningfully eroded by probate cost and time. For those families, the legislative answer is not coming. The practical answer, as it has been for decades, is trust planning done before it is needed.

I have spent over fifteen years advising California families on the intersection of business ownership, real estate, and estate transitions. What I have seen consistently: the hardest problems are the ones families did not know had solutions until it was too late to use them.

If you own a California home worth more than $750,000 without a trust, AB 2016 changed what you should be thinking about. If you are already navigating an inherited property, or considering whether trust planning might make sense for your family, an experienced California estate attorney can walk through the specific analysis for your situation.

The families who navigate this well tend to be the ones who understood their options before they needed them.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is California AB 2016?

California Assembly Bill 2016, effective April 1, 2025, raised the threshold for a simplified probate procedure from $184,500 to $750,000. The change applies only to a decedent's primary residence in California.

Who qualifies for the AB 2016 simplified probate procedure?

Four requirements must all be met: the property was the decedent's primary residence, the gross value is under $750,000, the death occurred on or after April 1, 2025, and at least 40 days have passed since the date of death.

Does AB 2016 apply to homes above $750,000?

No. Homes valued above $750,000 do not qualify for the simplified petition and must go through traditional formal probate unless the property was held in a trust before death.

Does AB 2016 affect Proposition 19 property tax reassessment?

No. AB 2016 addresses probate procedure only. Proposition 19 determines how inherited property will be taxed after title transfers. These are entirely separate issues.
