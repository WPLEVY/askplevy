Long-Term Care and the Family Home: Planning While Parents Are Still Living

Most of the legal and real estate issues that arise when a parent dies could have been addressed more effectively, and often at far lower cost, while the parent was still alive and able to make decisions. The family home sits at the center of that reality. Decisions made during a parent's lifetime about long-term care, reverse mortgages, title, and estate documents will determine what adult children actually inherit, and how cleanly.

This guide is for adult children who are in that planning window now, and for attorneys and advisors who work with them. It covers the intersection of long-term care planning and real property, specifically in California, where Medi-Cal rules, Proposition 19, and property tax considerations create a legal environment that does not always behave the way families expect.

A companion worksheet for structuring the family conversation is available at Parent Planning Worksheet.

Why This Conversation Belongs Before the Crisis

Planning discussions about the family home are uncomfortable precisely because they require acknowledging that a parent may lose capacity, need institutional care, or die. Families tend to defer these conversations until a crisis forces them. By then, options have narrowed significantly.

A parent who still has legal capacity can execute a trust, amend an existing trust, grant a power of attorney, make deliberate title decisions, review insurance, and choose how to fund future care. A parent who has lost capacity can do none of these things. The family is left managing with whatever documents exist, or with no documents at all, often through a conservatorship proceeding that is expensive, slow, and publicly visible.

The specific intersection of long-term care and real property is worth addressing as a distinct topic because the decisions compound. A reverse mortgage taken to fund care affects what heirs inherit. A gift of the home to a child to avoid Medi-Cal recovery may trigger a lookback disqualification. A parent added to a care facility's occupancy agreement may trigger the "permanently left the home" clause in a reverse mortgage. These interactions are not obvious and they are not well-explained by the institutions involved.

Medi-Cal and the Family Home

Medi-Cal, California's Medicaid program, covers long-term care for eligible individuals, including nursing home care that can cost $8,000 to $15,000 per month or more. For families whose primary asset is the home, the question of what Medi-Cal can recover from the estate is critical.

California substantially narrowed its Medi-Cal Estate Recovery Program (MERP) in 2017. Under current law, the state may only seek recovery from assets that pass through the probate estate. Property held in a revocable living trust, property passing by joint tenancy right of survivorship, or property with a beneficiary designation generally passes outside of probate and is not subject to Medi-Cal recovery at death.

See Welfare and Institutions Code § 14009.5; California Code of Regulations, Title 22, § 50960 et seq.

This makes proper trust planning the primary protective tool. A parent whose home is held in a revocable living trust at death will generally not have that home subject to a Medi-Cal recovery claim, regardless of how much the state paid for their care.

However, two important limitations apply. First, Medi-Cal can place a lien on real property during a recipient's lifetime in certain circumstances, including when the recipient is institutionalized and not expected to return home. This lien attaches to the property and must be satisfied even if the property passes outside of probate. Second, if the parent's home was not in a trust and passes through probate, the full recovery rules apply.

The practical implication. A parent who has not transferred their home into a revocable living trust before needing Medi-Cal-funded long-term care may leave their family in a significantly worse position than one who did, even if the cost of care was identical. Trust planning during the parent's lifetime, ideally well before care is needed, is the most effective tool available.

The 5-Year Lookback and Gifts of the Home

Families who learn that Medi-Cal cannot recover from trust assets sometimes conclude that the solution is to simply give the home to a child before applying for benefits. This approach runs directly into Medi-Cal's transfer-of-assets rules and the 5-year lookback period.

Under federal Medicaid law, incorporated into California's Medi-Cal program, a transfer of assets for less than fair market value within 60 months of applying for long-term care benefits creates a period of ineligibility for benefits. The ineligibility period is calculated by dividing the value of the transferred asset by the average monthly cost of nursing facility care in California.

See 42 U.S.C. § 1396p(c); Welfare and Institutions Code § 14006.3.

For a home worth $900,000 transferred to a child, and an average monthly nursing home cost of $12,000, the resulting ineligibility period would be 75 months. During that period, Medi-Cal will not pay for long-term care. If the parent actually needs care during that window, the family must fund it privately, which often means selling the very asset that was transferred to protect it.

There are limited exceptions to the transfer penalty. A transfer to a spouse is exempt. A transfer to a disabled child is exempt. A transfer to a child who lived in the home and provided care that delayed institutionalization for at least two years may be exempt under the caregiver child exception. These exceptions are narrow and fact-specific, and reliance on them requires careful documentation and legal guidance.

The overarching point is that gifting the home to a child is not a reliable Medi-Cal planning strategy unless done more than five years before the parent applies for benefits. Within the lookback window, it is likely to cause more problems than it solves.

Reverse Mortgages Taken to Fund Care

Reverse mortgages are frequently used to fund in-home care or to cover other expenses during a parent's later years. They allow a homeowner 62 or older to borrow against home equity without making monthly payments, with the loan becoming due when the borrower permanently leaves the home or dies.

When a parent takes a reverse mortgage to fund care at home, families need to understand several things that will affect what happens to the property later.

Permanent departure triggers repayment. A reverse mortgage becomes due when the borrower permanently leaves the home, including a move to an assisted living facility or nursing home. Temporary hospitalizations generally do not trigger repayment, but extended stays in a care facility do. The loan servicer's definition of "permanent" matters and varies somewhat by loan type.

The non-recourse protection. Reverse mortgages are non-recourse loans. If the loan balance exceeds the home's value at repayment, heirs can surrender the home to the lender without personal liability for the shortfall. This is a meaningful protection for families, but it means the home may produce no inheritance if the loan balance has grown to approximate or exceed the home's value.

The 12-month resolution window. When a borrower dies, heirs typically have up to 12 months to resolve the reverse mortgage: sell the home and use proceeds to repay the loan, refinance with a new loan to retain the home, or surrender the home to the lender. The 12-month period is not automatic and requires proactive communication with the servicer.

For a more detailed treatment of mortgage issues and inherited property, see our guide on what happens to a mortgage when you inherit a California home.

Interaction with Medi-Cal. A home subject to a reverse mortgage may still qualify as an exempt asset for Medi-Cal eligibility purposes while the parent intends to return home. But when the parent permanently leaves for a care facility, the reverse mortgage comes due, and the home is typically sold. The sale proceeds, net of the loan payoff, may affect Medi-Cal eligibility if they exceed the applicable asset limits. Families navigating both a reverse mortgage and Medi-Cal eligibility should get coordinated advice from an elder law attorney before the parent's move to a facility.

Long-Term Care Insurance and Home Equity

Long-term care insurance can cover the cost of nursing home, assisted living, or in-home care, reducing or eliminating the need to draw on home equity to fund care. For families whose primary concern is preserving the home for the next generation, a well-structured LTC policy is a meaningful planning tool.

Policies vary significantly in ways that matter. The benefit period determines how long the policy will pay. A three-year benefit period may be sufficient for the average care stay; longer periods cost more but protect against outlier situations. The daily or monthly benefit amount must be calibrated to actual local care costs, which in California are among the highest in the country. Inflation protection determines whether the benefit amount keeps pace with rising care costs over a policy's life. The elimination period is the number of days the insured must pay out of pocket before the policy begins paying, functioning like a deductible.

From a home-planning perspective, the key question is whether the policy will cover enough of the care cost to avoid drawing down home equity. A policy that covers a meaningful portion of a $12,000-per-month nursing home bill preserves options that a family without coverage does not have.

Families should review any existing LTC policy before a parent needs care, not after. Late-stage review often reveals lapses, benefit limitations, or elimination periods that the family had not anticipated. Confirming that premiums are current, understanding the claims process, and understanding what the policy will and will not cover are important steps in the pre-care planning conversation.

Adding a Child to Title: Why It Usually Backfires

One of the most common instincts when families discuss protecting the family home is to add an adult child to the deed. The logic is intuitive: if the child already owns part of the home, it cannot be taken from them. In practice, this approach creates more problems than it solves in almost every case.

The child's interest is exposed to the child's creditors. Once a child holds title to the property, their ownership interest can be reached by their creditors, subject to a judgment lien, or complicated by divorce proceedings. The parent has introduced the child's financial and personal circumstances into the chain of title.

The transfer may trigger Medi-Cal lookback issues. Adding a child to title as a co-owner is a transfer of an interest in the property. If the parent later applies for Medi-Cal within five years, this transfer will be scrutinized as a potential disqualifying transfer under the lookback rules.

The parent loses control. A co-owner has rights in the property. Depending on how title is held, the parent may no longer be able to sell, refinance, or encumber the property without the child's consent. If the relationship with that child changes, the parent's options are constrained.

The child loses the step-up in basis on the transferred share. Property a child inherits receives a stepped-up basis to fair market value at the parent's death, which can eliminate capital gains on a subsequent sale. Property a child receives as a gift during the parent's lifetime carries over the parent's original basis. For a home with significant appreciation, this difference can represent a substantial tax cost. See our guide on step-up in basis for California inherited property for a full explanation.

The alternative. A revocable living trust accomplishes the parent's actual goals, which are typically continuity of management during incapacity and avoidance of probate at death, without any of the drawbacks above. The parent retains full control during their lifetime, the property passes to named beneficiaries at death outside of probate, and beneficiaries receive the stepped-up basis. Adding a child to title is a shortcut that usually does not work as intended.

Powers of Attorney and Healthcare Directives

A durable power of attorney for finances and an advance healthcare directive are essential documents for managing a parent's affairs, including real property, during incapacity. They are not the same document and do not serve the same purpose.

A durable power of attorney for finances allows the named agent to manage the principal's financial affairs, including real property transactions, during incapacity. For the power to be useful in this context, it must be durable, meaning it expressly provides that it remains effective upon the principal's incapacity. A standard power of attorney that is not durable terminates automatically at incapacity, which is exactly when it would be needed most.

See California Probate Code § 4124 (durability requirement); Probate Code §§ 4400-4465 (Uniform Statutory Form Power of Attorney Act).

California's statutory power of attorney form was recently revised by AB 2199, signed June 30, 2026, to add successor agent designation, conservator nomination, and express digital asset authority. See our article on what changed under AB 2199 for detail on these additions.

An advance healthcare directive (also called a healthcare proxy or AHCD) designates a healthcare agent to make medical decisions and documents the principal's wishes regarding treatment, including end-of-life care. It does not authorize financial transactions. For property management during incapacity, the financial POA is the relevant document.

See California Probate Code §§ 4600-4805 (Power of Attorney for Health Care); Health and Safety Code § 1569.71 (residential care placement).

The practical planning point for families: both documents should be in place before a parent loses capacity. A parent who has not executed these documents when they become necessary will likely require a court-supervised conservatorship to manage their affairs, which is more expensive, slower, and more restrictive than private document-based planning.

Trust Planning as the Baseline

Throughout this guide, a revocable living trust has appeared repeatedly as the preferred structure for protecting the family home from both Medi-Cal recovery and probate. This section explains why it is the baseline, not an advanced option.

A revocable living trust holds title to the home while the parent is alive, with the parent typically serving as both trustor and trustee. The parent retains full control and can revoke or amend the trust at any time while they have capacity. At the parent's death, the successor trustee manages and distributes the property according to the trust's terms, without court involvement.

See California Probate Code §§ 15200-15212 (creation and validity of trusts); Probate Code § 15400 (revocability).

From a Medi-Cal perspective, a home held in a revocable living trust is not subject to estate recovery at death under current California law, because the property passes outside of probate. From an income tax perspective, the beneficiaries receive the stepped-up basis at the parent's death. From a property tax perspective, a transfer into a revocable living trust is generally not a change of ownership for reassessment purposes under California Revenue and Taxation Code Section 62(d).

See California Revenue and Taxation Code § 62(d) (revocable trust transfer not a change of ownership).

The trust does not insulate the home from a Medi-Cal lien during the parent's lifetime if the parent is institutionalized and the lien attaches under applicable regulations. But it does protect against estate recovery at death, which is where the larger risk typically lies.

For families where the parent has not yet established a trust, the planning conversation should prioritize this step above most others. For families where a trust exists, the conversation should confirm that the home is actually titled in the trust, since an unfunded trust offers no protection.

A Practical Conversation Guide for Adult Children

The obstacle to planning is rarely lack of information. It is the difficulty of having the conversation. Adult children are reluctant to raise topics that feel like they are discussing a parent's death or decline. Parents are sometimes reluctant to disclose financial details or to acknowledge the possibility of losing independence. Both responses are understandable and both produce worse outcomes than the alternative.

A few practical suggestions for structuring the conversation:

Frame it as organizing, not planning for death. The conversation is about making sure documents are in order, that someone knows where things are, and that the parent's wishes are documented while the parent can express them. This framing is accurate and less threatening than "we need to talk about what happens when you die."

Start with logistics, not assets. Asking a parent where they keep important documents, who their attorney is, and whether they have a trust or will is less emotionally loaded than asking what they plan to leave to whom. The logistical questions surface the substantive issues naturally.

Ask about the home specifically. Does the parent want the home sold after they die, or would they prefer a child to keep it? If a child were to keep it, which child, and how would the others be treated equitably? Is there a mortgage or reverse mortgage? These questions rarely get asked directly, and the parent's answers are often different from what the children assumed.

Address care funding before it becomes a crisis. Has the parent thought about how they would fund care at home or in a facility? Is there LTC insurance? Would they consider using home equity? Do they understand what Medi-Cal covers and what it does not? These questions are easier to discuss when care is theoretical than when a discharge planner is asking them in a hospital corridor.

Use a structured tool. Our Parent Planning Worksheet is designed for exactly this conversation. It covers property status, estate documents, care preferences, family communication, and documentation location in a format that can be completed together during a family meeting or shared ahead of time for a parent to complete at their own pace.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can Medi-Cal take my parent's home after they die?

California limits Medi-Cal estate recovery to assets that pass through probate. Property held in a revocable living trust or passing by other non-probate means generally avoids recovery. However, Medi-Cal can place a lien during the parent's lifetime in certain circumstances, and probate assets remain subject to recovery. Trust planning is the primary protective tool. See Welfare and Institutions Code § 14009.5.

What is the Medi-Cal 5-year lookback?

Medi-Cal's 5-year lookback applies to transfers of assets made for less than fair market value within 60 months of applying for long-term care benefits. Gifting the home within that window can result in a period of ineligibility calculated on the transferred value. See 42 U.S.C. § 1396p(c) and Welfare and Institutions Code § 14006.3.

Is adding a child to the deed a good way to protect the family home?

Rarely. It exposes the child's interest to their creditors, may trigger Medi-Cal lookback issues, reduces the parent's control, and eliminates the stepped-up basis on the transferred share. A revocable living trust almost always achieves the same goals with fewer risks.

What happens to the family home if a parent takes out a reverse mortgage?

The reverse mortgage becomes due when the parent permanently leaves the home or dies. Heirs have up to 12 months to sell, refinance, or surrender the property. Reverse mortgages are non-recourse, so heirs are not personally liable if the loan exceeds the home's value.

Does a power of attorney let a child manage the family home during incapacity?

Yes, if the POA is durable and grants real property authority. It must expressly state it remains effective during incapacity under Probate Code § 4124. A non-durable POA terminates at incapacity, which is when it would be needed most.
