Trust-Owned Property in California

By William B. Plevy · Attorney | California Real Estate Broker

Much of California's real estate is held in trusts. Revocable living trusts are among the most common estate planning tools, and families frequently transfer their homes, rental properties, and investment real estate into trusts during their lifetimes. When the trust settlor dies, that real property remains in the trust until the trustee sells it, transfers it to beneficiaries, or otherwise administers it.

Understanding how trust ownership affects real property, who can act, how title works, what process applies to a sale or transfer, and what the property tax and income tax implications are, is essential for anyone involved in California trust administration.

How Trust Ownership of Real Property Works

When real estate is held in a trust, the title is vested in the trustee in their fiduciary capacity, not in the trustee personally. A typical vesting might read: "Jane Smith, as Trustee of the Smith Family Living Trust dated January 1, 2010." The trust is not a separate legal entity in the same way a corporation is. Rather, the trustee holds the property in a fiduciary capacity for the benefit of the trust beneficiaries.

During the settlor's lifetime, a revocable living trust typically names the settlor as both the trustee and the primary beneficiary. This means the settlor retains full control. They can sell, refinance, or modify the trust or the property. From a practical standpoint, the property is treated much like personally owned property for the settlor's purposes.

Upon the settlor's death, the trust becomes irrevocable (or if it was already irrevocable, administration continues). The successor trustee named in the trust document steps in and has the authority and obligation to administer the trust assets, including the real property, in accordance with the trust terms.

Trustee Authority Over Real Property

A trustee's authority over trust real property comes from the trust document itself and from California law. Most well-drafted revocable living trusts give the trustee broad powers, including:

  • Power to hold, manage, and operate trust real property
  • Power to sell, lease, or otherwise dispose of real property
  • Power to make improvements and repairs
  • Power to borrow against real property (mortgage) if appropriate
  • Power to distribute real property to beneficiaries

Even absent specific language, California's Probate Code grants trustees a broad set of default powers. Trustees who are uncertain about their authority should review the trust document with legal counsel before taking significant action.

Authority has limits. A trustee must always act within the terms of the trust and in accordance with fiduciary duties. Even if the trust gives broad authority, the trustee cannot use that authority to benefit themselves at the expense of beneficiaries, or to take actions that are not in the beneficiaries' best interests.

Successor Trustee Issues

The succession of trustee authority after a settlor's death can be administratively complex. Several issues commonly arise:

Confirming the successor trustee's role. The trust document names one or more successor trustees. The successor trustee must accept the role, which is typically evidenced by a formal acceptance of trustee document. Until the successor trustee has accepted, they do not have authority to act.

Capacity issues with a co-trustee or prior trustee. If the prior trustee resigned, was removed, or became incapacitated rather than dying, the process for the successor trustee to establish authority may be slightly different. The trust document should address these scenarios.

Multiple co-trustees. If the trust names multiple co-trustees, they must generally act together unless the trust document authorizes individual action. This can create administrative complexity.

No qualified successor. If the named successor trustees are unwilling or unable to serve and the trust does not provide an adequate mechanism for appointing a replacement, a petition to the California probate court may be necessary to appoint a successor trustee.

Certification of Trust

When a trustee needs to transact business with banks, escrow companies, title companies, or other parties in connection with trust real property, those institutions typically will not review the entire trust document (which is often a confidential private document). Instead, they work from a Certification of Trust (also called an Affidavit of Trust).

A Certification of Trust is a document, typically prepared by an attorney and signed by the trustee under penalty of perjury, that summarizes key facts about the trust without revealing confidential distribution provisions. It typically includes:

  • The name and date of the trust
  • Identity of the current trustee(s)
  • Confirmation that the trust is still in existence
  • Confirmation that the trustee has authority to enter into the proposed transaction
  • Other relevant information depending on the transaction

California law (Probate Code § 18100.5) specifically provides for Certifications of Trust. Escrow and title companies in California routinely rely on them for trust real estate transactions. Trustees should obtain a Certification of Trust early in the administration process, as it will be needed for virtually every real property transaction.

Selling Trust Property

Selling California real property held in a trust involves most of the same steps as any other residential sale, with several trust-specific considerations:

Confirming authority. The Certification of Trust must confirm that the trustee has authority to sell the property. If the trust requires beneficiary consent for real estate sales, that consent must be obtained before listing.

Title vesting. Title is typically held as "Jane Smith, as Trustee of the Smith Family Living Trust dated January 1, 2010." The deed in a sale will transfer from the trustee in that capacity to the new buyer.

Escrow and title requirements. The title company will require a Certification of Trust, often a copy of the trust document or relevant portions, a death certificate if the settlor has died, and sometimes additional documentation to establish the chain of authority.

Disclosures. Trust sales are subject to California disclosure requirements, including a form of the Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS) specifically for trust, estate, and conservatorship transactions.

Income tax. The trustee should consult with a CPA about the income tax implications of the sale, including the applicable cost basis (typically a stepped-up basis for inherited property) and reporting obligations.

Transferring Property to Beneficiaries

A trustee may distribute trust real property to a beneficiary rather than selling it. This involves recording a deed transferring title from the trustee to the beneficiary. Several considerations apply:

Proposition 19. As discussed in the Proposition 19 guide, distributing real property to a beneficiary may trigger property tax reassessment depending on the relationship between the settlor and the beneficiary, the property type, and whether the beneficiary meets the requirements for an available exclusion.

Beneficiary consent and communication. Before distributing property to a beneficiary, the trustee should confirm that the distribution is consistent with the trust document and that beneficiaries have been informed of the Proposition 19 implications.

Equalization. When there are multiple beneficiaries and one receives property while others receive cash or other assets, the trustee should analyze whether the distribution is equitable and whether Proposition 19 affects the relative value of the property distribution.

Deed preparation. The deed must be properly prepared and recorded. An incorrect deed can cause significant title problems. In California, deeds transferring real property out of a trust typically must describe the beneficiary's interest and may need to include a preliminary change of ownership report (PCOR) to give the county assessor notice of the transfer.

Property Tax Reassessment and Proposition 19

Property tax reassessment is one of the most financially significant issues in California trust real estate. The key principles:

  • Transfers between a settlor and their own revocable trust do not trigger reassessment.
  • The settlor's death does not itself trigger reassessment of property in a revocable trust.
  • When a trustee distributes property to a beneficiary after the settlor's death, that distribution may trigger reassessment, depending on the relationship between the parties and the type of property.
  • Under Proposition 19 (effective February 16, 2021), the parent-child exclusion from reassessment is available only for a parent's principal residence transferred to a qualifying child who moves in. Rental, investment, and vacation properties are generally subject to full reassessment when distributed from parent to child.

The reassessment analysis should be completed before any distribution is made. A distribution that triggers reassessment cannot easily be undone, and the property tax consequence can be significant and permanent.

LLC and Entity Ownership

Some California families hold real estate in LLCs or other entities rather than directly in a trust. This can create a more complex picture when administering the estate.

If the LLC interests (not the real property directly) are held in the trust, the trustee may be administering LLC membership interests rather than directly administering real property. The trustee's authority to sell, distribute, or otherwise deal with the LLC is governed by both the trust document and the LLC operating agreement.

Several issues arise with entity-owned real property in trust administration:

  • Change in ownership rules. Transfers of LLC membership interests can trigger California property tax reassessment under the change-in-ownership rules applicable to legal entities, which are distinct from the individual transfer rules. The analysis is fact-specific and professional guidance is important.
  • Parent-child exclusion not available. Property held in an LLC generally does not qualify for the Proposition 19 parent-child exclusion from reassessment.
  • Operating agreement requirements. The LLC's operating agreement may impose restrictions on transfer of membership interests, require manager or member consent for certain actions, or have other provisions that affect the trustee's ability to act.
  • Dissolution or continued operation. The trustee may face decisions about whether to continue operating the LLC or to wind it up and distribute the property directly. Each option has different legal, tax, and operational implications.

Common Mistakes in Trust Real Property Administration

  • Failing to re-title the property after the settlor's death (leaving it in the deceased person's name creates title problems)
  • Allowing insurance to lapse due to change in occupancy
  • Distributing property without analyzing Proposition 19 reassessment consequences
  • Failing to obtain a Certification of Trust before attempting real estate transactions
  • Making decisions without understanding the trust document's requirements and limitations
  • Allowing a beneficiary to occupy trust property without a written agreement
  • Selling trust property without providing required disclosures
  • Failing to account to beneficiaries for trust real estate transactions

Trust-Owned Property Administration Checklist

  • Locate and review the trust document
  • Confirm successor trustee acceptance and authority
  • Obtain a Certification of Trust from legal counsel
  • Review current title vesting and confirm property is in the trust
  • Obtain homeowner's insurance update and confirm coverage
  • Continue mortgage and property tax payments
  • Obtain appraisal of property
  • Consult with CPA on income tax basis and reporting
  • Analyze Proposition 19 before any distribution
  • Communicate with beneficiaries about property plans
  • If selling: engage broker, prepare disclosures, follow California sale process
  • If distributing: prepare deed with PCOR and record properly
  • Provide beneficiaries with accounting of trust real estate transactions

Frequently Asked Questions

Does property in a trust avoid probate?

Yes, generally. Property held in a properly funded revocable living trust at the time of the settlor's death passes to beneficiaries through trust administration rather than through probate court. This avoids the time and expense of California probate, which is one of the primary reasons people establish living trusts.

What happens if the property was never transferred into the trust?

If the settlor created a trust but never transferred the real property into it (a common occurrence), the property is not in the trust and does not pass through trust administration. Depending on the value and circumstances, a probate proceeding or a simplified court process may be required to transfer title.

Can a trustee sell trust property quickly?

A trustee with proper authority can sell trust property relatively efficiently compared to a probate sale, which requires court approval. However, the trustee must still obtain proper documentation, engage qualified professionals, satisfy disclosure requirements, and act consistent with fiduciary duties. A realistic timeline for a trust real estate sale, once the trustee is qualified and the property is prepared, is similar to a conventional sale.

Does the trust get a step-up in basis at the settlor's death?

Generally, yes. Property held in a revocable living trust typically receives a stepped-up cost basis to fair market value at the settlor's death under federal income tax law. This means that a sale of the trust property shortly after death may result in minimal capital gains. Consult a CPA for your specific situation.

What if there are two trustees and they disagree?

If the trust requires co-trustees to act together and they cannot agree, administration can be deadlocked. Options may include one trustee petitioning for removal of the other, seeking court instructions, or mediation. The specific trust document language will affect what options are available.
