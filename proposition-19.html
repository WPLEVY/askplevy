Proposition 19 and California Family Property Transfers

By William B. Plevy · Attorney | California Real Estate Broker

California Proposition 19, which took effect in key parts on February 16, 2021, fundamentally changed how many California families approach property transfers between generations. Before Proposition 19, California's parent-child and grandparent-grandchild exclusions allowed broad reassessment protection for inherited and transferred real property. After Proposition 19, those protections were significantly narrowed.

The result is that families with valuable California real estate, including appreciated homes, rental properties, vacation properties, farmland, and commercial real estate, need to plan more carefully than before. Transfers that once happened as routine paperwork now carry meaningful property tax consequences if not structured correctly or timed thoughtfully.

This guide explains what Proposition 19 changed, how the rules work today, and the planning considerations families, trustees, and property owners should understand.

Background: How California Property Tax Worked Before Proposition 19

California property taxes are governed primarily by Proposition 13, passed in 1978, which limits annual increases in assessed value to 2% per year. When real property changes ownership, it is generally reassessed to current market value, which can dramatically increase the annual property tax bill for high-appreciation California real estate.

Before Proposition 19, Propositions 58 (1986) and 193 (1996) created broad parent-child and grandparent-grandchild exclusions from reassessment. Under the old rules, a parent could transfer their principal residence to a child without reassessment regardless of value. Additionally, up to $1 million of assessed value (not market value) in other real property could be transferred from parent to child without triggering reassessment. This meant many rental properties, vacation homes, and commercial real estate could pass to the next generation at the parent's low assessed value.

For California families with appreciated real estate, especially in coastal areas where values had grown dramatically over decades, this was a significant financial benefit. A parent with a house assessed at $200,000 but worth $2 million could transfer it to a child, who would continue paying taxes on the $200,000 assessed value rather than on the current market value.

What Proposition 19 Changed

Proposition 19, approved by California voters in November 2020, substantially narrowed the parent-child and grandparent-grandchild exclusions from property tax reassessment. The key changes affecting family transfers took effect February 16, 2021.

The core changes:

  • Principal residence only: The exclusion is now limited to a parent's principal residence transferred to a child who also makes it their principal residence.
  • Value cap applied: Even for qualifying principal residences, if the current market value exceeds the parent's assessed value by more than $1 million, the excess triggers a partial reassessment.
  • No rental property exclusion: The former $1 million assessed-value exclusion for non-principal-residence property is gone. Rental properties, vacation homes, commercial real estate, and farmland transferred from parent to child are now subject to full reassessment.
  • Occupancy requirement: The transferee child must move into the property as their principal residence within one year of transfer.
Key takeaway: The main impact of Proposition 19 is on rental, investment, vacation, and commercial property. Families who previously expected to pass these assets to children at low assessed values now face substantial reassessment at transfer, often resulting in significantly higher annual property tax obligations.

The Parent-Child Exclusion Under Proposition 19

Under Proposition 19, the parent-child exclusion from reassessment applies only when:

  1. The property being transferred is the parent's principal residence at the time of transfer;
  2. The child who receives the property makes it their own principal residence within one year of the transfer; and
  3. The child files the required application with the county assessor.

If these conditions are met, the exclusion applies, but it is now a partial exclusion rather than a full one. The assessed value is adjusted as follows: if the property's fair market value at transfer is less than or equal to the parent's assessed value plus $1 million, then no reassessment occurs. If the fair market value exceeds that threshold, the assessed value is set at fair market value minus $1 million.

For example: if a parent's home has an assessed value of $400,000 and a fair market value of $2 million at the time of transfer, and a child qualifies for and claims the exclusion, the new assessed value would be approximately $1 million (the fair market value minus $1 million). That is not the parent's $400,000 assessed value, but not the full $2 million either. This is a partial benefit, but still significant for many California families.

Principal Residence Issues

The word "principal residence" carries legal weight under Proposition 19. Both the parent and the child must satisfy principal residence requirements: the parent at the time of transfer, and the child after the transfer.

What qualifies as a principal residence? The California Board of Equalization and county assessors look at factors including: where the person is registered to vote, where they file income taxes, the address on their driver's license, actual physical occupancy, and the intent with which the property is held. It is not simply a question of putting the address on a form.

The child's one-year deadline to move in is strictly interpreted. If the child does not establish the transferred property as their principal residence within one year, the exclusion is lost entirely and the property is reassessed at full market value as of the transfer date.

A few additional nuances worth understanding:

  • If a parent owned multiple residences, only one can be the principal residence for purposes of the Proposition 19 exclusion.
  • A rental dwelling that a parent temporarily vacated at or near death is generally not considered a principal residence for Proposition 19 purposes.
  • Properties held in LLCs, partnerships, or other entities generally do not qualify for the parent-child exclusion.

Rental and Investment Property Under Proposition 19

This is where the impact of Proposition 19 is most severe. Under the prior rules, up to $1 million in assessed value (not market value) of non-principal-residence property could be transferred from parent to child without reassessment. That protection is gone.

Rental properties, investment properties, vacation homes, commercial properties, and agricultural land transferred from parent to child are now subject to full reassessment at current market value. For a rental property in a coastal California market that was purchased decades ago, this can mean an increase in assessed value from hundreds of thousands of dollars to millions, and a corresponding increase in annual property taxes that dramatically changes the economics of holding the property.

Many California families are discovering that rental properties inherited before Proposition 19 had an implied subsidy baked in: low assessed values that had been protected from reassessment for decades. Proposition 19 eliminates that for future transfers.

This creates real planning urgency for families who anticipated transferring rental properties to children. The window for planning before Proposition 19 closed on February 16, 2021. Families now work within the new framework.

Grandparent-Grandchild Transfers

The grandparent-grandchild exclusion under Proposition 19 is similarly narrowed. The exclusion now requires that all of the following be true:

  • The parents of the grandchild who would normally be eligible for the parent-child exclusion are deceased;
  • The grandchild makes the transferred property their principal residence within one year; and
  • The property was the grandparent's principal residence.

In practice, this means the grandparent-grandchild exclusion applies in a narrow set of circumstances: where both parents are deceased, the property was the grandparent's principal residence, and the grandchild moves in. Outside of those conditions, grandparent-to-grandchild transfers are generally subject to full reassessment.

Trust Distributions and Proposition 19

Many California families hold real estate in revocable living trusts, and the question of how Proposition 19 applies to trust distributions is important and not always intuitive.

The key principle is that the Proposition 19 exclusions apply based on the legal transfer of property, not just on how the property is titled. When a trustee distributes real property from a trust to a beneficiary after the trust settlor's death, that distribution is typically treated as a "change in ownership" that triggers reassessment analysis.

Whether an exclusion applies depends on the relationship between the original property owner and the receiving beneficiary, whether the receiving beneficiary meets the principal residence requirements, and whether the timing and mechanics of the transfer satisfy the requirements for claiming the exclusion.

Several common trust distribution scenarios:

  • Principal residence distributed to a child who moves in: May qualify for a partial exclusion if requirements are met.
  • Rental property distributed to a child: Generally triggers full reassessment.
  • Property distributed to a beneficiary who is not a qualifying child: Generally triggers full reassessment.
  • Property distributed to a surviving spouse: Spousal transfers are generally excluded from reassessment (this was not changed by Proposition 19).
Practice point: Trustees should not distribute real property from a trust without first understanding the Proposition 19 implications of that distribution. The timing, manner, and recipient of a distribution can all affect whether and how reassessment applies. Consultation with qualified legal and tax advisors before distribution is strongly advisable.

Trustee Considerations Under Proposition 19

Trustees administering California trusts that hold real property carry significant responsibility under Proposition 19. Several considerations apply:

Fiduciary duty to consider tax consequences. Trustees generally owe beneficiaries a duty of prudent administration, which includes understanding the tax and assessment consequences of their decisions. A trustee who distributes property without considering Proposition 19 implications may face questions from beneficiaries about whether the fiduciary duty was properly discharged.

Communicate with beneficiaries before distributing. Trustees should inform beneficiaries of the potential Proposition 19 implications before distributing real property. A beneficiary who expects to receive a rental property with a low assessed value should understand that the distribution may trigger full reassessment.

Consider whether sale is preferable to distribution. In some cases, a trust may be better served by selling a property and distributing cash proceeds rather than distributing the property to a beneficiary who will face immediate reassessment. This analysis depends on the property's appreciated value, the beneficiaries' tax situations, and their plans for the property.

Document the analysis. Trustees should document their review of Proposition 19 issues as part of the administration record. This protects the trustee from later claims that the analysis was not conducted.

Sibling Equalization Issues

Sibling equalization arises when multiple children inherit an estate and some inherit real property while others inherit cash or other assets. Under the prior rules, the parent-child exclusion applied broadly, so distributing appreciated real property to one child was relatively tax-neutral from a property tax standpoint. Under Proposition 19, things are more complicated.

For example: if a trust holds a rental property worth $3 million and cash worth $3 million, and there are two children, a trustee might consider distributing the property to one child and the cash to another. But under Proposition 19, the child who receives the property will face reassessment (since it is not a principal residence). This changes the economics of the distribution and may affect what is "equal" from the perspective of each beneficiary.

Trustees should factor Proposition 19 reassessment risk into equalization analysis, and consider obtaining professional appraisals and tax analysis before making decisions about how property is distributed among beneficiaries.

Common Mistakes Before Recording a Deed

Several mistakes recur with enough frequency that they deserve specific attention:

  • Transferring property without a tax analysis. Families sometimes complete a transfer by preparing and recording a deed without consulting a tax or legal advisor. By the time the reassessment notice arrives, the transfer is done and the opportunity to plan around it has passed.
  • Assuming the child will qualify for the exclusion. The child must actually move in, within one year, and must file the required claim with the county assessor. Failing to follow through on these steps means losing the exclusion even if the property was transferred to the right person.
  • Overlooking entity-owned property. Property owned by an LLC or partnership does not automatically qualify for the parent-child exclusion. Transferring LLC membership interests rather than real property directly may have different, and potentially worse, consequences than expected.
  • Failing to coordinate with estate planning. Proposition 19 should be part of any estate plan review that involves California real property. Estate plans prepared before Proposition 19 may not account for the new rules.
  • Missing the claim deadline. The claim for exclusion must be filed with the county assessor within the required timeframe. Missing the deadline can result in full reassessment that could have been partially avoided.

Planning Connections: Estate Planning, Trusts, and Inherited Real Estate

Proposition 19 does not exist in isolation. It intersects with estate planning, trust drafting, trust administration, probate, and the practical reality of California real estate ownership.

Clients who hold appreciated California real estate should consider Proposition 19 as part of:

  • New estate plans: Trusts and wills can be drafted to reflect Proposition 19 realities, for example, building in flexibility for trustees to sell or distribute depending on the beneficiary's ability to qualify for the exclusion.
  • Existing plan reviews: Plans prepared before February 2021 may not account for the new rules and should be reviewed.
  • Trust administration: Trustees should conduct Proposition 19 analysis before every real property distribution.
  • Probate: Executors and administrators distributing real property through probate face the same Proposition 19 issues.
  • Lifetime transfers: Families sometimes consider making transfers during a parent's lifetime. Whether this helps or hurts under Proposition 19 depends on the property type, the family's goals, and other planning considerations.

Practical Checklist: Proposition 19 and California Property Transfers

  • Identify all California real property in the estate or trust and categorize by type (principal residence vs. rental/other)
  • Determine current assessed value and current market value for each property
  • Identify all potential beneficiaries and their relationship to the transferor
  • Assess whether any qualifying child could and would move into the principal residence
  • Calculate potential reassessment exposure for each property under Proposition 19
  • Consider whether selling the property and distributing cash may be more advantageous than distributing the property
  • Review trust documents to understand trustee authority and any distribution instructions
  • Consult with legal counsel and a CPA before recording any deed or making any distribution
  • If the exclusion is pursued, ensure the claim is filed timely with the county assessor
  • Document the Proposition 19 analysis in the trust or estate administration record

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Proposition 19 eliminate the parent-child exclusion entirely?

No. Proposition 19 significantly narrowed the parent-child exclusion, but it did not eliminate it. For a parent's principal residence transferred to a child who moves in, a partial exclusion may still apply. The exclusion for rental, investment, and non-principal-residence property is essentially gone.

When did Proposition 19 take effect?

The provisions affecting parent-child and grandparent-grandchild transfers took effect February 16, 2021. Transfers completed before that date were subject to the prior rules.

Does Proposition 19 apply if property passes through a trust?

Yes. Distributing real property from a trust to a beneficiary after the settlor's death generally triggers the same Proposition 19 analysis as a direct transfer. Whether an exclusion applies depends on the relationship, the property type, and whether the qualifying conditions are met.

What is the one-year rule for moving into the property?

Under Proposition 19, a child who receives a parent's principal residence must establish the property as their own principal residence within one year of the date of transfer. Missing this deadline generally results in loss of the exclusion and full reassessment at market value.

Does Proposition 19 affect the step-up in basis at death?

Proposition 19 is a property tax rule, not an income tax rule. The step-up in cost basis for inherited property is governed by federal income tax law and is a separate issue. However, families often need to consider both property tax and income tax consequences when planning property transfers, since they interact in important ways. A CPA or tax attorney should be consulted for income tax analysis.

Can a trust distribution be structured to minimize Proposition 19 exposure?

The mechanics and timing of a distribution can affect Proposition 19 outcomes in some circumstances, but there is no simple workaround. Distributions of non-qualifying property will generally be subject to reassessment. The most important step is conducting the analysis before making the distribution, so that all options can be considered, including whether a sale is preferable.

What if the property is held in an LLC?

Property held in an LLC does not generally qualify for the Proposition 19 parent-child exclusion. Transfers of LLC membership interests may trigger reassessment in a different way (through the change-in-ownership rules applicable to legal entities). The analysis is fact-specific and professional guidance is important.

Does Proposition 19 apply to commercial real estate?

Yes. Commercial real estate inherited or transferred from parent to child is subject to full reassessment under Proposition 19, as it is not a principal residence eligible for the remaining exclusion.
