Inherited California Real Estate

By William B. Plevy · Attorney | California Real Estate Broker

Inheriting real estate in California can be one of the most significant financial events in a person's life—and one of the most complicated. Property does not transfer the way a bank account does. The questions multiply quickly: Who has authority to act? Does this go through probate? What happens to the mortgage? Can the property be sold? What do the other heirs want?

This guide walks through the key issues that arise when California real estate is inherited, whether through a trust, probate, or joint tenancy. It is meant to help beneficiaries, trustees, heirs, and family members understand the landscape and make informed decisions.

First Steps After Inheriting Property

The period immediately following a death is often overwhelming. But there are practical steps that should be taken relatively quickly when real property is involved:

  • Secure the property. If the property is vacant, ensure it is physically secured, locked, and monitored if possible. Vacant properties face risks including vandalism, unauthorized entry, and deferred maintenance issues that can escalate.
  • Notify the insurance company. Many homeowner's policies have vacancy exclusions or reduced coverage when a property is unoccupied. The insurance company should be notified of the owner's death and the occupancy status of the property. Failure to do so can leave the property underinsured or uninsured.
  • Continue paying the mortgage. If the property has a mortgage, payments should continue. Mortgage servicers may offer temporary arrangements in the context of death, but unpaid mortgage payments accumulate interest, late fees, and can lead to default proceedings.
  • Continue paying property taxes. California property taxes are due in two installments. Missing payments results in penalties and interest. The county tax collector generally does not distinguish between living and deceased owners until notified.
  • Locate and review the estate planning documents. Whether there is a will, a trust, a deed with right of survivorship, or no planning documents at all will determine the path forward and who has authority to act.

Trust vs. Probate: Understanding the Difference

Whether California real estate passes through a trust or through probate is one of the most important threshold questions. The answer affects the timeline, the cost, who has authority, and what steps are required.

Trust administration occurs when the property was held in a trust (typically a revocable living trust) at the time of death. The successor trustee named in the trust document takes over after the settlor's death and administers the trust according to its terms, including distributing or selling the real property. Trust administration is a private process and generally does not require court supervision, though it still has its own requirements and formalities.

Probate is the court-supervised process for administering a deceased person's estate when property was not held in trust, did not pass by beneficiary designation, and was not held in joint tenancy. California probate court proceedings are public, take time (often a year or more for a typical estate), and involve statutory fees. For a significant estate with real property, probate fees can be substantial.

Joint tenancy with right of survivorship is a form of co-ownership in which the surviving co-owner(s) automatically inherit the deceased owner's interest. Title passes outside of probate and outside of a trust, simply by recording an affidavit of death of joint tenant along with a death certificate.

Understanding which of these applies to the property at hand determines nearly everything about the process that follows.

Who Has Authority to Act

Authority to act on behalf of a deceased person's estate is a critical threshold question. Until someone has proper legal authority, they generally cannot sell the property, enter into contracts on its behalf, or take most other significant legal actions.

  • Trustee: If the property is in a trust, the successor trustee named in the trust document has authority to act once they have accepted the role, typically evidenced by a Certification of Trust and accompanying documentation.
  • Executor or Administrator: If the property is in probate, the executor (named in the will) or administrator (appointed by the court when there is no will) has authority once Letters Testamentary or Letters of Administration are issued by the court.
  • Surviving Joint Tenant: A surviving joint tenant can record an Affidavit of Death of Joint Tenant with a certified death certificate to establish title in their name.
  • No one yet: In some cases, no one has yet been formally appointed or qualified to act. In these situations, steps must be taken to establish authority before meaningful decisions about the property can be made.

Title Review: Understanding How the Property Is Held

Before any decision is made about inherited real property, the title should be reviewed. A title review typically involves obtaining the current title report or examining the recorded documents in the county recorder's office to understand:

  • How the property is vested (in whose name, in what form of ownership)
  • Whether there are any liens (mortgage, tax liens, judgment liens, mechanic's liens)
  • Whether there are any easements, encumbrances, or restrictions
  • Whether there are any title defects or issues that would need to be resolved before a sale

Title surprises are not uncommon in inherited property. The deceased may have taken out a reverse mortgage, refinanced without everyone's knowledge, had a judgment lien recorded against them, or held title in a way that does not match what family members expected. Discovering these issues early allows for planning and problem-solving before they become deal-killers in a sale.

Practical Responsibilities: Insurance, Mortgage, Taxes, Utilities, and Repairs

While legal formalities are being addressed, the physical property continues to exist and require attention. Key practical considerations:

Homeowner's insurance. As noted, vacancy can affect coverage. If the property will be vacant for an extended period, a vacant property insurance policy or endorsement may be needed. Trustees and executors have a fiduciary duty to maintain adequate insurance coverage on estate assets.

Mortgage. If the deceased had a mortgage, the loan is a lien against the property that must be addressed. Mortgage servicers are required to work with successors and estates under applicable federal regulations, but the obligation to make payments does not disappear. The trustee or executor should contact the servicer early to understand options.

Property taxes. California property taxes are a lien against the property that must be paid. The county tax collector is generally not aware of a death until notified. Missing tax installment deadlines results in 10% late penalties.

Utilities. Utilities should generally be maintained in a vacant property to prevent freezing pipes, mold, and other damage. The person with authority over the estate should maintain utility service and arrange for periodic property inspections.

Repairs and maintenance. Trustees and executors are generally expected to maintain the property in reasonable condition. Deferred maintenance can reduce the property's value and may expose fiduciaries to claims by beneficiaries. Major repairs should be documented and, in some cases, approved by beneficiaries or the court before proceeding.

Selling vs. Keeping Inherited Property

One of the most significant decisions facing heirs and trustees is whether to sell the inherited property or retain it. This decision involves multiple considerations:

Income tax considerations. Inherited property generally receives a step-up in cost basis to fair market value at the date of death (discussed further below). This means that a sale shortly after death may result in little or no capital gains tax. However, retaining the property and selling it years later may result in a larger gain and higher tax obligation. The income tax picture is an important part of the decision.

Property tax considerations (Proposition 19). If the property is to be distributed to a beneficiary rather than sold, Proposition 19 reassessment issues must be analyzed. For a rental or investment property, distribution to a child will trigger full reassessment at market value.

Market conditions. The real estate market is a relevant factor. Timing a sale in the context of estate administration involves balancing legal and administrative timelines with market conditions.

Beneficiary preferences. When multiple beneficiaries are involved, their preferences and financial situations affect the decision. Some beneficiaries may want cash; others may want to keep the property. If consensus cannot be reached, legal mechanisms exist to force resolution, including partition actions.

Condition of the property. Inherited properties are sometimes in deferred maintenance condition. The estate may need to make repairs or improvements before a sale, which requires authority, budget, and contractor access.

When Siblings Inherit Property Together

Co-ownership of inherited real estate among siblings is one of the most common sources of family conflict in estate administration. What begins as a shared inheritance can turn into a prolonged dispute when siblings disagree about what to do with the property.

Common issues that arise in sibling co-ownership situations:

  • One sibling wants to sell; another wants to keep. Without agreement, neither can force the outcome by themselves—but a partition action can compel a sale through court proceedings.
  • One sibling lives in the property. When a sibling occupies the inherited home, questions arise about whether they owe rent to co-owners, who pays expenses, and when the property might be sold.
  • Management and maintenance disagreements. Who makes decisions about repairs, expenses, and improvements? Who has access? Who handles the rental income if the property is rented?
  • Buyout negotiations. Often the most practical resolution is for one sibling to buy out the others. This requires agreement on value (typically established by appraisal) and the buying sibling's ability to finance the purchase.

Early communication, clear documentation, and sometimes a formal co-ownership agreement can prevent or contain these disputes. When co-owners are unable to agree, partition proceedings are available but expensive and disruptive for all involved.

Step-Up in Basis: The Income Tax Picture

For most inherited California real estate, the recipient receives what is called a "stepped-up" cost basis equal to the fair market value of the property at the date of the owner's death. This is a federal income tax concept with significant practical implications.

What it means in plain terms: if a parent purchased a home decades ago for $200,000 and it is worth $2 million at their death, the heir's cost basis is generally $2 million. If the heir sells the property shortly after inheriting it for $2 million, there may be little or no capital gains tax because the selling price equals the stepped-up basis.

This is a significant benefit of inherited property compared to gifted property (gifts carry over the donor's original cost basis) or property sold during life (where the seller's original purchase price is the basis). The step-up in basis is one reason many estate planning advisors suggest holding appreciated assets until death rather than gifting them.

The step-up rules are established under federal income tax law and have their own exceptions and nuances. Consulting a CPA or tax attorney for specific advice is essential, as the rules can be complex in situations involving trusts, community property, joint tenancy, and other forms of ownership.

Note: The step-up in basis is an income tax concept, not a property tax concept. It is separate from Proposition 19 property tax reassessment. Both issues arise at death and both affect the economics of inherited real estate, but they are governed by different laws and operate independently.

Proposition 19 and Inherited Property

When California real property is inherited, Proposition 19 may apply to limit or eliminate the property tax exclusion that was available under prior law. In general:

  • A parent's principal residence may qualify for a partial exclusion from reassessment if the inheriting child moves in and claims the exclusion.
  • Rental properties, investment properties, vacation homes, and other non-principal-residence properties are generally subject to full reassessment when they pass from parent to child under current law.

For a detailed discussion of Proposition 19, see the Proposition 19 and California Family Property Transfers guide.

Trustee and Executor Issues

When a trustee or executor is responsible for administering inherited real property, they face specific legal duties and practical challenges:

  • They must act in the best interest of all beneficiaries, not just themselves.
  • They must maintain and protect the property during the administration period.
  • They should not allow personal interests to conflict with fiduciary duties.
  • They should communicate regularly with beneficiaries about the status of the property and the administration.
  • For trustee responsibilities specifically, see the Trustee Responsibilities guide.

Common Mistakes

  • Failing to secure and insure the property immediately. The window between death and formal administration can leave the property vulnerable.
  • Assuming the property is clearly whose it is. Title must be verified, not assumed based on family conversations or expectations.
  • Making improvements or spending estate funds without authority. Trustees and executors who spend estate money on unauthorized improvements may face personal liability.
  • Delaying probate or trust administration. Delay increases costs, creates property risk, and can damage family relationships.
  • Failing to consider Proposition 19 before distributing. A distribution that triggers reassessment cannot be undone.
  • Overlooking liens and encumbrances. Inherited property may have debt that reduces net value significantly.
  • Allowing sibling disputes to fester. Prolonged co-ownership disputes consume time, money, and family relationships.

Practical Checklist: Inherited California Real Estate

  • Secure and inspect the property
  • Notify the homeowner's insurance company of the death and occupancy status
  • Identify whether the property passes through trust, probate, or joint tenancy
  • Locate and review estate planning documents
  • Establish proper legal authority (Letters, Trustee certification)
  • Review the title report for liens and encumbrances
  • Continue mortgage payments and property tax payments
  • Obtain appraisal of the property value for estate and tax purposes
  • Communicate with co-heirs or beneficiaries about plans and timeline
  • Consider Proposition 19 implications before making any distribution
  • Consult with legal and tax professionals before selling or distributing
  • Document all decisions and transactions

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to go through probate to transfer inherited California real estate?

It depends on how the property was held. If it was in a trust, joint tenancy, or had a recorded transfer-on-death deed, probate can often be avoided. If the property was held in the deceased person's name alone and was not in a trust, probate is generally required unless the estate qualifies for a simplified procedure under California law.

Can I sell inherited property right away?

Generally, you need to have proper legal authority before selling. If the property is in a trust, the trustee can sell it once qualified. If it is in probate, the executor or administrator needs to follow California procedures for selling estate property. The timeline depends on the administration process.

What happens if one heir refuses to agree to sell?

If inherited property is held in co-ownership and a co-owner refuses to agree to a sale, the other co-owners can file a partition action in California court. The court can order a sale even over the objection of a non-consenting co-owner, though this process can be expensive and time-consuming.

Do I owe income taxes when I inherit California real estate?

Inheriting property typically does not itself create an immediate income tax event. However, when you sell the property, any gain above the stepped-up basis is subject to capital gains tax. California has no estate tax of its own, and there is no federal inheritance tax. Consulting a CPA for your specific situation is recommended.

What is a Certification of Trust and when do I need one?

A Certification of Trust (also called an Affidavit of Trust) is a document that summarizes the key provisions of a trust and establishes the trustee's authority without requiring disclosure of the full trust document. Banks, escrow companies, and title companies typically require a Certification of Trust before working with a trustee on real estate matters.

How is the property taxed under Proposition 19 when it is inherited?

If the inherited property was the deceased person's principal residence and the inheriting child moves in within one year and claims the exclusion, a partial reassessment exclusion may apply. For rental properties, investment properties, and vacation homes, full reassessment to current market value is generally triggered.
