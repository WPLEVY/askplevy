Probate Real Estate in California

By William B. Plevy · Attorney | California Real Estate Broker

When a California property owner dies without a trust, or without transferring all of their property into a trust, and the estate does not qualify for a simplified small estate process, the real estate must generally pass through California probate court. Probate real estate—property that is part of a probate proceeding—operates under specific rules that affect how, when, and by whom the property can be sold or transferred.

Understanding the California probate process for real estate helps families, heirs, and advisors set realistic expectations, avoid costly mistakes, and navigate the administration more efficiently.

What Is Probate Real Estate

Probate is the court-supervised process for administering the estate of a deceased person. California probate is handled in the Superior Court of the county where the deceased person resided. "Probate real estate" refers to real property that is an asset of a probate estate—meaning it must be administered through this court process before it can be sold or transferred to heirs or beneficiaries.

Not all real property at death goes through probate. Property that avoids probate includes:

  • Property held in a living trust (passes through trust administration)
  • Property held in joint tenancy with right of survivorship (passes to surviving joint tenant by affidavit and death certificate)
  • Property with a recorded revocable transfer-on-death deed (passes to named transferee)
  • Community property with right of survivorship (passes to surviving spouse)

Property that is in the deceased person's name alone, without any of the above arrangements, is generally subject to California probate if the total value of probate assets exceeds $184,500 (as of recent adjustments; this threshold periodically changes).

Establishing Authority to Act

No one has automatic authority to sell or transfer probate real estate. The personal representative of the estate—either the executor named in the will or an administrator appointed by the court when there is no will—must be formally appointed by the California probate court before they can act on behalf of the estate.

The process of being appointed typically involves:

  1. Filing a petition for probate with the appropriate Superior Court
  2. Setting a hearing date (typically six to eight weeks after filing)
  3. Providing notice to heirs and creditors
  4. Having the court formally appoint the executor or administrator at the hearing
  5. Receiving Letters Testamentary (if there is a will) or Letters of Administration (if there is no will) from the court

Until Letters are issued, the personal representative cannot enter into a contract to sell the property, and no title company will insure a sale of probate real estate without them.

Letters Testamentary and Letters of Administration

Letters Testamentary and Letters of Administration are court-issued documents that grant the personal representative authority to act on behalf of the estate. They are essentially the personal representative's "credentials" that allow them to transact with banks, title companies, and other parties.

Letters have practical limitations:

  • They are typically issued in limited quantities and must be obtained from the court.
  • Many institutions require Letters issued within a certain period (often 60 to 90 days) as "current" Letters.
  • Letters may expire or need to be updated if the administration takes a long time.

Personal representatives should obtain multiple certified copies of Letters at the outset of administration, as they will need them for real property transactions, bank accounts, and other administrative tasks.

Independent Administration of Estates Act (IAEA)

The California Independent Administration of Estates Act (IAEA) allows personal representatives to administer many estate transactions without court supervision, as long as proper notice is given to interested parties and no objection is raised. This is an important tool for simplifying probate real estate sales.

Under IAEA, a personal representative who is granted independent administration authority can generally:

  • List and market the property for sale
  • Accept an offer to purchase the property
  • Enter into a purchase and sale agreement
  • Close the sale without court confirmation hearing

However, IAEA requires that notice of the proposed action be given to all interested parties (heirs, devisees, creditors) and that they have an opportunity to object. If no objection is filed within the notice period, the transaction can proceed without court confirmation. If an objection is filed, a court hearing may be required.

Whether IAEA authority applies in a particular estate depends on what authority was requested in the petition and granted by the court at the outset of the proceeding.

Court Confirmation of Sales

When IAEA authority is not available or has not been obtained, a sale of probate real estate in California requires court confirmation. Court confirmation involves a hearing at which:

  • The personal representative presents the proposed sale to the court
  • The court may receive overbids from other interested buyers
  • The court confirms the sale to the highest bidder

The overbidding process is a distinctive feature of court-confirmed probate sales in California. Once a purchase price is established, other buyers can appear at the confirmation hearing and bid a minimum of 10% over the first $10,000, plus 5% of the remainder, above the original offer price. This can result in the property selling to a buyer other than the one who originally had the property under contract.

Court confirmation adds time to the sale process, typically several additional weeks or more, and introduces pricing uncertainty. Buyers should be aware of the overbid process before committing to a probate sale under court confirmation procedures.

The Sale Process for Probate Real Estate

Selling probate real estate in California involves several steps:

  1. Appoint a personal representative and obtain Letters from the court.
  2. Obtain an appraisal of the property. In probate, the court-appointed probate referee (a licensed appraiser) typically values the property for estate purposes. A separate market appraisal or broker opinion may also be obtained for pricing purposes.
  3. Engage a real estate broker. The broker should have experience with probate or estate sales, which have specific disclosure and procedural requirements.
  4. List the property. Probate real estate is listed on the MLS like any other property, though the listing will typically indicate the property's probate status.
  5. Accept an offer. Under IAEA, the personal representative can accept an offer. Under court confirmation, the offer begins the court confirmation process.
  6. Provide disclosures. California's disclosure requirements apply to probate sales, including the estate version of the Transfer Disclosure Statement and other required disclosures.
  7. Complete IAEA notice or court confirmation process as applicable.
  8. Close escrow. The personal representative signs closing documents in their fiduciary capacity. Title transfers from the estate to the buyer.
  9. Account for and distribute proceeds. Sale proceeds are accounted for in the estate and eventually distributed to heirs and beneficiaries after payment of estate debts, expenses, and taxes.

Occupancy During Probate

Probate properties are sometimes occupied by a surviving family member or tenant during the administration. This creates several issues:

Family member occupancy. A surviving spouse or family member living in the probate property is common. The personal representative must balance their duty to administer the estate with the occupant's interests. If the occupant has no legal right to remain, they may need to be advised to vacate before a sale can proceed.

Tenant occupancy. If the property is a rental and has tenants, those tenants have rights under California landlord-tenant law that must be respected. The personal representative cannot simply ask tenants to leave. Required notices and procedures must be followed.

Insurance and condition. Whether or not the property is occupied, it should be insured and maintained during the administration. Vacant properties may require special insurance coverage.

Repairs and Maintenance During Probate

Personal representatives are expected to maintain estate property in reasonable condition. The key considerations are similar to trust administration:

  • Emergency repairs should be made promptly and documented
  • Routine maintenance should continue
  • Major repairs or improvements typically require court authorization or, if IAEA authority exists, beneficiary notice
  • All expenditures should be documented with receipts and reported in the estate accounting

Personal representatives who spend estate funds on repairs that benefit one heir more than others, or who make expenditures that are not in the estate's interest, may face objections from creditors or beneficiaries.

Beneficiaries and Heirs in Probate Real Estate

Heirs and beneficiaries have standing as interested parties in a California probate proceeding. They are entitled to notice of major estate transactions, including the sale of real property. They can object to proposed sales, and their objections may result in court hearings.

The personal representative's duty is to all interested parties, not just to the most vocal heir. When beneficiaries disagree about whether to sell the property, at what price, or on what terms, the personal representative must navigate these disputes while fulfilling their fiduciary obligations.

Common beneficiary issues in probate real estate:

  • One heir wants to buy the property from the estate at below-market value
  • Heirs disagree about whether to sell now or wait for a higher price
  • An heir is living in the property and objecting to a sale
  • Creditor claims against the estate affect the net proceeds to beneficiaries

Timeline Considerations

California probate takes time. A straightforward probate with a real estate sale can often be completed in approximately 9 to 18 months, depending on the court's calendar, the complexity of the estate, any contested matters, and the real estate market. More complex estates can take significantly longer.

Key timeline considerations for probate real estate:

  • The property cannot typically be sold until Letters are issued (6-8 weeks after filing, at minimum)
  • Under IAEA with no objections, a sale can close within the normal escrow period after the offer is accepted
  • Under court confirmation, add several weeks for the confirmation hearing
  • Creditor claim periods must run before final distribution can be made
  • If the estate has income tax or estate tax obligations, those must be resolved before final distribution

Buyers of probate real estate should be aware that the timeline is less flexible than a conventional sale and plan accordingly.

Common Mistakes in Probate Real Estate

  • Attempting to sell the property before Letters are issued
  • Failing to obtain an independent appraisal for pricing purposes
  • Selecting a broker who lacks probate and estate sale experience
  • Failing to provide required disclosures to buyers
  • Not communicating with beneficiaries about the sale, leading to objections
  • Making unauthorized repairs or improvements to the property
  • Selling to a related party without full disclosure and proper procedures
  • Failing to account for sale proceeds properly in the estate accounting

Probate Real Estate Checklist

  • File for probate and petition for executor/administrator appointment
  • Obtain Letters Testamentary or Letters of Administration
  • Request IAEA authority if appropriate
  • Secure and insure the property immediately
  • Continue mortgage and property tax payments
  • Obtain probate referee appraisal and market appraisal
  • Notify heirs and beneficiaries of property plans
  • Engage a qualified real estate broker with estate sale experience
  • Prepare required disclosures
  • Follow IAEA or court confirmation process as applicable
  • Close escrow and account for proceeds
  • Include real estate transactions in the final estate accounting

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take to sell a house in California probate?

From the date of death to closing, a probate real estate sale often takes 9 to 18 months in a straightforward case, though it can take longer depending on estate complexity, court scheduling, and any contested matters. The property can typically be listed once Letters are issued (about 2 months after filing) and can be sold under IAEA relatively quickly thereafter if no objections arise.

What is the difference between an executor and an administrator in California probate?

An executor is named in the decedent's will to administer the estate. An administrator is appointed by the court when there is no will or no qualified executor available. Both roles carry similar fiduciary duties and authority, though the source of their appointment differs.

Can an heir buy the probate property?

An heir can purchase estate property, but this transaction requires careful handling due to the fiduciary context. The purchase should generally be at fair market value established by appraisal, with proper notice to all interested parties, and with full transparency. Court involvement may be required or advisable depending on the circumstances.

Does probate real estate need to be sold as-is?

Not necessarily. Probate real estate can be prepared for sale with appropriate repairs and staging, subject to the personal representative's authority and fiduciary obligations. However, many probate sales are sold as-is due to the complexity of managing repairs during administration and the condition issues often present in estate properties.

Can creditors make claims on probate real estate proceeds?

Yes. The debts of the decedent—including mortgages, judgment liens, and other claims—must be paid from the estate, including from real estate sale proceeds, before the balance is distributed to beneficiaries. The personal representative must review claims filed against the estate and address them appropriately before making distributions.
