Family Property Transfers in California

By William B. Plevy · Attorney | California Real Estate Broker

Transferring real estate within a family in California sounds simple. A parent wants to give a home to a child. Siblings want to divide inherited property. A family wants to put their rental properties into an LLC. In practice, each of these transactions involves a constellation of issues: property tax reassessment, income tax consequences, mortgage due-on-sale clauses, title mechanics, lender approval, gift tax considerations, and the potential for family disagreement.

This guide explains the key types of family real estate transfers in California, what issues each raises, and what families and their advisors should consider before recording a deed or signing a transfer document.

Parent-to-Child Transfers

Transferring real estate from a parent to a child is one of the most common family property transactions. It can occur during the parent's lifetime as a gift or sale, or at death through trust administration or probate. Each version has different legal, tax, and property tax consequences.

Lifetime gifts. A parent can deed property to a child during their lifetime. From a gift tax standpoint, transfers above the annual exclusion and lifetime exemption may require filing a federal gift tax return, though most individuals do not owe actual gift tax because of the large lifetime exemption. From an income tax standpoint, the child receives the parent's cost basis (carryover basis)—not the stepped-up basis that would apply if the child inherited the property at the parent's death. This is a significant difference for appreciated property.

At death through trust or probate. Property transferred at death through a trust or probate generally receives a stepped-up cost basis to fair market value. The income tax consequence of a subsequent sale can be much more favorable than a lifetime gift.

Proposition 19 and property taxes. Under Proposition 19 (effective February 16, 2021), a lifetime or at-death transfer of a parent's principal residence to a child who moves in may qualify for a partial property tax reassessment exclusion. For rental, investment, and vacation property, full reassessment generally applies. See the Proposition 19 guide for details.

Key planning point: For many families with appreciated California real estate, there is a tension between transferring property now (capturing income tax basis planning) and waiting until death (gaining a stepped-up basis but potentially losing property tax exclusion if Proposition 19 applies). This analysis is fact-specific and should be done with legal and tax counsel.

Sibling-to-Sibling Transfers

Sibling-to-sibling transfers of California real property arise most commonly when siblings inherit property together and one sibling wants to buy out the other(s). They can also arise when siblings want to restructure inherited co-ownership or when a sibling who did not receive certain property wants to acquire a share.

Key issues in sibling transfers:

  • Property tax reassessment. Transfers between siblings are not eligible for the Proposition 19 parent-child exclusion. A sibling-to-sibling transfer generally triggers reassessment of the transferred interest at current market value.
  • Valuation. To establish a fair buyout price, a professional appraisal is recommended. This avoids disputes and provides documentation for accounting and tax purposes.
  • Financing. The buying sibling may need to refinance or obtain a new mortgage to fund the buyout. Lender requirements and approval processes apply.
  • Partition as an alternative. If siblings cannot agree on a buyout price or terms, a partition action can force a sale or division of the property through court proceedings.

Transfers to a Trust

Transferring real property into a revocable living trust is one of the most common estate planning steps in California. The transfer is accomplished by recording a new deed vesting title in the trustee in their fiduciary capacity ("John Smith, as Trustee of the Smith Living Trust dated..."). From a property tax perspective, transfers from an individual to their own revocable living trust do not trigger reassessment. The transfer is treated as a non-event for property tax purposes.

Several practical points about trust transfers:

  • The deed must be properly prepared and recorded to effectively transfer the property into the trust.
  • The lender holding any mortgage on the property should generally be notified, though federal law (the Garn-St. Germain Act) generally protects against enforcement of due-on-sale clauses when property is transferred into a revocable trust where the borrower is and remains a beneficiary.
  • Homeowner's insurance should be updated to reflect the trust as an additional insured or named insured.
  • For California property, a Preliminary Change of Ownership Report (PCOR) is filed with the county recorder at the time of recording.

Transfers from a Trust

When property is distributed from a trust to a beneficiary after the settlor's death, the deed transfers title from the trustee in their fiduciary capacity to the beneficiary. As discussed in the Proposition 19 section, this distribution may trigger property tax reassessment depending on the relationship between the settlor and the beneficiary and the type of property.

Additional considerations for trust-to-beneficiary transfers:

  • The trustee must have authority under the trust document to make the distribution.
  • The transfer may create income for the beneficiary if the trust had deferred income, and should be coordinated with the trust's accountant or CPA.
  • The deed should be prepared by legal counsel to ensure it properly reflects the transfer and includes required California documentation.

Transfers to an LLC or Entity

Some families transfer real property into an LLC or other entity for liability protection, management simplicity, or estate planning purposes. This is a significant transaction with multiple implications:

Property tax reassessment risk. In California, transfers of real property to an entity such as an LLC are generally subject to the change-in-ownership rules. However, a transfer from an individual to an LLC in which that individual holds a majority ownership interest may qualify for an exclusion from reassessment, depending on the structure. The rules are complex and the analysis is fact-specific.

Due-on-sale risk. If the property has a mortgage, the lender's due-on-sale clause may be triggered by a transfer to an LLC. Many residential mortgage lenders do not permit transfer to an LLC without their consent. Some commercial lenders are more flexible. Lender approval should be obtained before the transfer is recorded.

Proposition 19 and future transfers. Property held in an LLC does not qualify for the Proposition 19 parent-child exclusion. Once property is in an entity, transfers of membership interests or future planning around the property must account for the entity ownership layer.

Title insurance. Title insurance on the property does not automatically transfer when the property is conveyed to an LLC. New title insurance may be needed, and the transfer may affect pending title claims.

Gift, Basis, and Income Tax Issues

Several income tax and gift tax concepts are relevant to family property transfers:

Gift tax. Federal gift tax applies to transfers of property for less than full value. California does not have a state gift tax. The federal annual exclusion allows individuals to give up to a certain dollar amount per year per recipient without filing a gift tax return (the amount adjusts periodically). Gifts above the annual exclusion require filing a gift tax return (Form 709), though actual gift tax is typically not owed unless the cumulative lifetime gifts exceed the large federal lifetime exemption.

Carryover basis for gifts. Property received as a gift during the donor's lifetime retains the donor's original cost basis (adjusted for improvements and depreciation). If a parent paid $100,000 for a home that is now worth $1.5 million and gives it to a child during their lifetime, the child takes the $100,000 basis. If the child later sells for $1.5 million, they may owe substantial capital gains tax.

Stepped-up basis at death. Property inherited at death generally receives a stepped-up cost basis to fair market value at the date of death. This is a major advantage of inherited property over gifted property for income tax purposes.

California income tax. California taxes capital gains as ordinary income. There is no preferential capital gains rate at the state level, unlike the federal system. This increases the income tax cost of selling appreciated California real estate.

Property Tax Reassessment

California's Proposition 13 limits annual increases in assessed value, but reassessment to current market value occurs on a "change in ownership." Family transfers are subject to the change-in-ownership rules, subject to available exclusions:

  • Spousal exclusion: Transfers between spouses are generally excluded from reassessment.
  • Parent-child exclusion (Proposition 19): Now limited to a parent's principal residence transferred to a qualifying child who moves in. Rental and investment property is generally reassessed.
  • Grandparent-grandchild exclusion: Available in limited circumstances under Proposition 19.
  • Trust transfers: Transfer to the settlor's own revocable trust is not a change of ownership. Trust distributions to beneficiaries are analyzed based on the settlor-beneficiary relationship.

Before any family transfer, the property tax reassessment analysis should be completed. In many cases, the annual property tax increase from reassessment is the single most significant financial consequence of the transfer.

Title and Deed Mechanics

Transferring real property in California requires recording a deed with the county recorder in the county where the property is located. Key elements of a properly executed deed:

  • Identifies the grantor (current owner) and grantee (new owner) clearly
  • Contains a legal description of the property
  • Is signed by the grantor (and notarized)
  • Includes a Preliminary Change of Ownership Report (PCOR)
  • Is recorded with the county recorder

Common deed types in California include grant deeds (the most common in residential transfers) and quitclaim deeds (often used for ownership adjustments between family members). The deed type matters for the warranties of title conveyed.

Mistakes in deed preparation can create title defects that are expensive and time-consuming to resolve. Deeds should be prepared by legal counsel or under legal supervision, especially for family transactions where the stakes are significant.

Mortgage and Lender Issues

If the property has a mortgage, the lender has an interest in any transfer. Most standard mortgage documents contain a due-on-sale clause that technically allows the lender to call the loan due and payable if the property is transferred without the lender's consent. However:

  • Under federal law (the Garn-St. Germain Depository Institutions Act), certain transfers cannot trigger the due-on-sale clause, including transfers to a spouse or child where the transferee will occupy the property as their principal residence, and transfers to a revocable living trust where the borrower is and remains a beneficiary.
  • For other transfers (to LLCs, to distant family members, transfers that change occupancy status), the lender may enforce the due-on-sale clause.
  • Even in protected transfers, notifying the lender and obtaining written acknowledgment is advisable to avoid administrative problems later.

Family Agreements and Buyouts

Family property transfers often involve negotiations among family members, including buyout arrangements. Well-structured family agreements can prevent disputes and provide a clear roadmap for ownership transitions. Elements of a well-structured family property agreement may include:

  • The agreed purchase price, established by professional appraisal
  • Payment terms (lump sum, installment payments, promissory note secured by deed of trust)
  • Transfer and closing timeline
  • Responsibility for closing costs
  • What happens if one party cannot perform
  • Representation and warranty provisions

Family property agreements should be in writing, reviewed by each party's legal counsel, and executed before the deed is recorded. Verbal agreements about real property are generally not enforceable in California.

Common Mistakes in Family Property Transfers

  • Recording a deed without understanding the property tax reassessment consequences
  • Giving appreciated property during the donor's lifetime when waiting for death would provide a much more favorable income tax basis
  • Transferring property to an LLC without lender consent, triggering due-on-sale enforcement
  • Using a poorly prepared deed that creates title defects
  • Failing to update homeowner's insurance after a transfer
  • Making family agreements verbally without written documentation
  • Failing to file required PCOR with the county assessor
  • Not consulting a CPA before executing a significant family property transfer

Family Property Transfer Checklist

  • Identify the type of transfer (gift, sale, death transfer, trust distribution)
  • Obtain a property appraisal to establish fair market value
  • Analyze property tax reassessment consequences under current law
  • Consult a CPA on gift tax, income tax basis, and income tax consequences
  • Review the mortgage documents for due-on-sale provisions
  • Obtain lender approval or confirm Garn-St. Germain protection applies
  • Prepare a written agreement if the transfer is a sale or has payment terms
  • Have the deed prepared by legal counsel
  • Include the Preliminary Change of Ownership Report (PCOR) with the deed
  • Record the deed with the county recorder
  • Update homeowner's insurance to reflect new ownership
  • Notify the lender in writing of the transfer

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I just add my child to the title of my house?

You can, but doing so has significant consequences that deserve careful consideration: it may trigger a partial property tax reassessment, it gives your child current ownership rights in the property, it uses a carryover basis (not a stepped-up basis) for any future sale, and it may affect your ability to refinance or sell without your child's cooperation. Consultation with legal and tax advisors before adding a co-owner is strongly recommended.

Does transferring my home to my trust affect my mortgage?

Generally not. Under the Garn-St. Germain Act, a transfer of a residential property to a revocable living trust where the borrower remains a beneficiary is protected from due-on-sale enforcement. However, notifying the lender and keeping them informed is advisable, and some lenders will want to review and acknowledge the trust documentation.

Is there California gift tax on family property transfers?

California does not have a state gift tax. At the federal level, transfers below the annual exclusion amount do not require a gift tax return, and most individuals will not owe actual gift tax even for larger gifts because of the large lifetime exemption. However, filing a gift tax return to document the transfer is often advisable even when no tax is owed.

What is a quitclaim deed and when should it be used?

A quitclaim deed transfers whatever interest the grantor has in the property, with no warranties of title. It is often used for transfers between family members, transfers to correct title issues, or transfers where the parties have a high level of trust and the warranty of title is not critical. In California, quitclaim deeds can trigger property tax reassessment just like grant deeds—the deed type does not affect the property tax analysis.

Can I sell my house to my child for $1?

Technically, a transfer can occur for any consideration, including $1. However, the IRS and California may treat a transfer below fair market value as part gift, with the corresponding income tax and gift tax implications. For property tax purposes, a transfer for less than full value generally triggers reassessment just as a gift would. A professional appraisal and tax advice are important before executing a below-market family sale.
