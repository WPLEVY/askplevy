What Happens to a Mortgage When You Inherit a California Home

Inheriting a California home is often an emotionally charged event, and the practical questions start immediately. One of the first is almost always about the mortgage. What happens to it? Who is responsible for it? Does it have to be paid right away? Can it be transferred?

The answers are more nuanced than most people expect, and they depend on the type of loan, how the property is held, and what the heir intends to do with it. This guide walks through the key issues from the perspective of someone who has seen these situations from both the legal and real estate sides.

Personal Liability: Are You on the Hook?

The first thing to understand is the difference between personal liability for a mortgage and the mortgage as a lien on the property.

When you inherit a home, you generally do not automatically become personally liable for the mortgage. Personal liability means the lender can sue you personally and pursue your other assets if the mortgage is not paid. That obligation belongs to the person who signed the loan documents. Unless you co-signed the original loan or separately agree to assume it, the lender cannot pursue you personally for the debt.

However, the mortgage is a lien on the property. The lender has a security interest in the home itself. If the mortgage is not paid, the lender can foreclose on the property regardless of who holds title. You may not be personally liable, but the home can still be lost to foreclosure if payments stop.

This distinction matters practically. It means an heir has choices about whether to take on the mortgage personally or simply manage the property as an asset while deciding what to do with it. But it also means ignoring the mortgage is not a viable strategy. Payments must continue to protect the property.

The Due-on-Sale Clause and Federal Protections

Most conventional mortgages include a due-on-sale clause, which gives the lender the right to demand full repayment of the loan when ownership of the property changes. In theory, this clause could allow a lender to call the entire mortgage due when a borrower dies and the property transfers to an heir.

In practice, federal law limits this significantly. The Garn-St. Germain Depository Institutions Act of 1982 prohibits lenders from enforcing due-on-sale clauses in several specific situations involving transfers at death, including:

  • A transfer to a relative upon the death of the borrower
  • A transfer where a spouse or child of the borrower becomes an owner of the property
  • A transfer resulting from a devise or descent (inheritance through a will or intestacy)
  • A transfer to an inter vivos trust in which the borrower is and remains a beneficiary

In plain terms: if you are inheriting a parent's home, the lender generally cannot call the loan due solely because of the death and transfer. You can take over the property and continue making payments without triggering the due-on-sale clause, even if the loan is not formally assumed in your name.

Important limitation. The Garn-St. Germain protection does not mean you can ignore the lender indefinitely. It means the lender cannot demand immediate repayment solely because of the transfer at death. If payments fall behind, or if the heir eventually sells the property to a third party, different rules apply. The protection is specifically for heirs inheriting and continuing to hold the property.

Mortgage Payments During Probate or Trust Administration

When a property owner dies, the property does not transfer to heirs instantaneously. It goes through a period of estate administration, either probate or trust administration, that can take months to years depending on the circumstances. During this period, the mortgage must still be paid.

In a probate estate, the executor or administrator is responsible for managing estate assets and paying estate obligations, including the mortgage. Mortgage payments come from estate funds. If the estate has insufficient liquid funds to cover payments while probate proceeds, the executor may need to petition the court for authority to sell the property or explore other options. Letting the property go into default during probate creates significant complications and can reduce the value available to heirs.

In a trust administration, the successor trustee holds the property and has the obligation to manage it responsibly for the benefit of the beneficiaries. That includes continuing mortgage payments from trust funds. The trustee cannot simply stop paying because the grantor has died.

In both cases, heirs who intend to keep the property should move quickly to understand the loan balance, the monthly payment, and the source of funds available to cover it during administration. Servicers will typically work with estates and trustees once proper documentation of authority is provided.

Your Options After Inheriting

Once the property transfers to you as heir, you have several paths depending on what you want to do with the home.

Continue making payments without formally assuming the loan. Under the Garn-St. Germain protections described above, you can continue making payments on the existing loan in the deceased borrower's name while you decide what to do. Many heirs do this for months or even years. The lender cannot call the loan due solely because of the death. However, the loan remains in the decedent's name, which can create complications if you need to refinance, modify the loan, or sell the property.

Formally assume the loan. You can contact the servicer and request to formally assume the loan in your own name. This puts you on the loan personally and gives you full borrower rights, including the ability to refinance or modify. Lenders are not always required to agree to assumption on conventional loans, but many will work with heirs on this, particularly if the heir qualifies under standard underwriting criteria.

Refinance the loan. If you intend to keep the property long term and want the loan in your own name with your own terms, refinancing is often the cleanest path. You apply for a new loan, pay off the inherited mortgage, and hold title and the loan in your name. This requires qualifying under current underwriting standards, which may be more or less favorable than the existing loan terms.

Sell the property. If you do not intend to keep the home, selling is straightforward. The mortgage is paid off from sale proceeds at closing. The stepped-up basis you received at inheritance generally means little or no capital gains tax if the sale occurs near the date of death value.

Allow the lender to foreclose. If the property is worth less than the mortgage balance, heirs may choose to simply allow foreclosure. Because you are not personally liable for the mortgage (assuming you did not co-sign), the lender's recourse is limited to the property itself. This is a significant advantage of inherited property compared to property you purchased with your own mortgage.

Loan Assumption

Not all loans are equally assumable. The rules depend on the loan type.

FHA loans are assumable, subject to the heir qualifying under FHA underwriting guidelines and the lender approving the assumption. FHA loans are common for first-time buyers and often have favorable interest rates, making assumption attractive when the existing rate is lower than current market rates.

VA loans are also assumable, though the rules differ from FHA. A non-veteran can assume a VA loan, but doing so may affect the original veteran's entitlement in ways that matter if the veteran's estate or surviving spouse has other VA loan activity. The VA loan benefit tied to the property is released when the assumption is completed and the property is no longer owned by a veteran.

Conventional loans typically contain due-on-sale clauses that prohibit traditional assumption. However, the Garn-St. Germain protections mean that heirs can continue payments without formal assumption, and many lenders will work with heirs on assumption or modification even for conventional loans, particularly in inherited property situations.

USDA loans have specific rules on assumption that vary. If the property was financed with a USDA rural development loan, direct contact with the servicer is necessary to understand what is permitted.

Reverse Mortgages

A reverse mortgage is a loan available to homeowners 62 or older that allows them to borrow against their home equity without making monthly payments. The loan balance grows over time as interest accrues. The loan becomes due and payable when the borrower dies, sells the home, or permanently moves out.

Inheriting a home with a reverse mortgage presents a different set of issues than inheriting a home with a conventional mortgage.

When the borrower dies, the loan servicer must be notified within 30 days. From that point, the estate or heirs generally have up to 12 months to resolve the loan, with extensions possible. The options are:

  • Sell the home and use the proceeds to pay off the reverse mortgage balance
  • Refinance with a new conventional loan to pay off the reverse mortgage and keep the home
  • Pay off the reverse mortgage from other estate funds or personal funds
  • Deed the property to the lender in lieu of foreclosure
  • Allow foreclosure if the home is worth less than the loan balance

Reverse mortgages are non-recourse loans. If the home is worth less than the loan balance at the time of the borrower's death, the heirs can walk away without personal liability. They simply lose the equity in the home, but they do not owe the difference. This is sometimes called the non-recourse protection and is a significant feature of reverse mortgages from an inheritance planning perspective.

Time matters with reverse mortgages. The 12-month resolution period is a real deadline. Servicers will begin foreclosure proceedings if the estate does not take action to resolve the loan within that window. Heirs who need time to sell, refinance, or make decisions should communicate proactively with the servicer and document all communications. Extensions are sometimes available but are not guaranteed.

Working with the Loan Servicer

The loan servicer is the company that manages the mortgage account, collects payments, and handles communications with the borrower. When a borrower dies, the servicer becomes an important point of contact for the estate and heirs.

To work effectively with the servicer after inheriting a property, you will typically need to provide:

  • A certified copy of the death certificate
  • Documentation establishing your authority over the estate or trust (Letters Testamentary, a Certification of Trust, or an Affidavit of Successor Trustee)
  • Identification and contact information for the heir or authorized representative

Once the servicer has this documentation, they can discuss the loan status, payment history, and available options. Federal regulations require servicers to work with successors in interest, which includes heirs who inherit property. The servicer cannot legally refuse to communicate with an heir who has properly established their interest in the property.

Keep written records of all communications with the servicer. Document when you called, who you spoke with, and what was said. If the servicer provides information about your options in writing, retain those documents. Loan servicers are large organizations and information can be inconsistent across departments. A clear paper trail protects you.

Frequently Asked Questions

Am I personally responsible for a mortgage on a home I inherit?

Not automatically. Inheriting a home does not make you personally liable for the mortgage unless you signed the loan documents or affirmatively assume the loan. However, the mortgage is a lien on the property. If it is not paid, the lender can foreclose regardless of who holds title.

Can a lender call the mortgage due when someone dies?

Most mortgages contain a due-on-sale clause, but federal law under the Garn-St. Germain Act prohibits lenders from enforcing it in most inheritance situations. If you are inheriting from a relative, the lender generally cannot demand immediate repayment solely because of the death and transfer.

Does the mortgage have to be paid during probate?

Yes. Mortgage payments must continue during probate to prevent default and foreclosure. The estate is responsible for making payments from estate funds while the property remains an estate asset.

Can I assume a mortgage on inherited property?

It depends on the loan type. FHA and VA loans are generally assumable subject to lender approval. Conventional loans typically prohibit formal assumption, but the Garn-St. Germain protections allow heirs to continue payments without formally assuming. Contact the servicer directly to understand your options.

What is a reverse mortgage and what happens when someone inherits a home with one?

A reverse mortgage becomes due when the borrower dies. Heirs typically have up to 12 months to sell the home, refinance, or pay off the loan. Reverse mortgages are non-recourse loans, so if the home is worth less than the balance, heirs can walk away without personal liability.
