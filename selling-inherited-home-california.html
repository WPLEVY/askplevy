Selling an Inherited Home in California: Timeline and Process

Selling an inherited California home is not like selling a home you purchased yourself. The legal pathway is different, the authority to sell must be established rather than assumed, the tax picture requires a different analysis, and the property often needs attention before it can be listed. Families who understand the process from the start move through it with less friction and fewer surprises.

This guide covers the full arc from the first decisions after inheriting to closing, with particular attention to the legal steps that are specific to inherited property in California.

Establishing Legal Authority to Sell

Before anything else, the person selling the property must have legal authority to do so. Who has that authority depends entirely on how the property is held.

Trust-owned property. If the property was held in a revocable living trust, the successor trustee has authority to sell it. To exercise that authority, the trustee needs to have properly assumed the role, typically by executing an Affidavit of Successor Trustee and obtaining a death certificate. The trustee will also need a Certification of Trust, a document that summarizes the trust's key terms and the trustee's authority without disclosing the full trust. Escrow and title companies require this documentation before they will work with a successor trustee. A trust sale does not require court involvement, which is one of the primary advantages of trust planning.

Probate property. If the property was held in the decedent's individual name and does not qualify for a simplified procedure, it goes through formal probate. The executor named in the will, or an administrator appointed by the court when there is no will, has authority to sell estate property. That authority is documented in Letters Testamentary or Letters of Administration issued by the probate court. The extent of that authority, specifically whether court confirmation is required for a sale, depends on whether the executor has Independent Administration of Estates Act authority.

AB 2016 simplified procedure. For primary residences valued under $750,000 where the death occurred on or after April 1, 2025, California's new simplified petition procedure may allow transfer without full probate. Once the property transfers to the heir through this process, the heir sells it as an owner with full authority. See our guide to AB 2016 for more detail.

Heirs who have received a distribution. Once probate or trust administration concludes and title transfers to the heir, the heir owns the property outright and can sell it without any special process.

Title Review

Before listing the property, a preliminary title report should be ordered. Title companies will run this as part of opening escrow, but reviewing it early can surface issues that need resolution before the property goes on the market.

Common title issues in inherited property situations include:

  • Liens or judgments against the decedent that attach to the property
  • Mortgages or deeds of trust that must be paid off or assumed
  • Property tax delinquencies
  • Easements or encumbrances that affect marketability
  • Title defects arising from how prior transfers were documented
  • Gaps in the chain of title if the trust was not properly funded during the decedent's lifetime

A trust that was never properly funded is a common and significant issue. If the decedent created a trust but never transferred the property into it, the property is not technically trust property and cannot be sold by the successor trustee. It must go through probate instead. This is one of the most common mistakes in estate planning and one that creates real delays and costs for heirs.

Tax Considerations Before You List

Two tax issues deserve attention before a sale: capital gains and property tax.

Capital gains and stepped-up basis. Inherited California real estate generally receives a stepped-up basis equal to the fair market value at the date of the decedent's death. If the property is sold near that value, capital gains are minimal or zero. The longer the time between the date of death and the sale, the more the property may have appreciated above the stepped-up basis, resulting in taxable gain. Heirs who want to minimize capital gains should get an appraisal as of the date of death early in the process and consider the timing of the sale relative to that basis. See our step-up in basis guide for a full explanation.

Proposition 19 and property tax. The sale itself does not trigger a property tax reassessment issue. Proposition 19 reassessment occurs when the property is inherited and title transfers to the heir, not at the subsequent sale. However, if the property has been reassessed upward due to Proposition 19, the increased property taxes during any holding period add to the cost of waiting to sell. Understanding the current assessed value and the resulting property taxes is part of the sell-or-keep analysis.

California income tax on capital gains. California taxes capital gains as ordinary income at the state level. There is no preferential long-term capital gains rate in California. On a property with significant appreciation above the stepped-up basis, the combined federal and California tax on the gain can be substantial. A tax advisor should model the numbers before the property is listed if there is meaningful appreciation above the date-of-death value.

Property Condition and Disclosures

California law requires sellers to disclose known material defects affecting the property. The Transfer Disclosure Statement and Seller Property Questionnaire are standard forms required in almost all residential sales. For inherited property, sellers often have limited direct knowledge of the property's condition, particularly if the decedent lived alone and the heirs were not involved in the home's maintenance.

Limited knowledge does not eliminate the disclosure obligation. Sellers must disclose what they actually know. Representations that turn out to be false, even if made in good faith, can give buyers grounds to pursue claims after closing. The safest approach for most inherited property sales is to order a pre-listing inspection and incorporate the findings into the disclosure package. This also allows the seller to decide whether to make repairs or price the property to reflect known issues.

Trustees and executors selling in a fiduciary capacity have their own disclosure forms and are held to a somewhat different standard than a typical owner-seller, but the obligation to disclose known defects remains. Buyers are always entitled to a full inspection contingency period regardless of how the property is sold.

Properties that have been occupied for many years, or that have deferred maintenance, may need attention before listing. Common items in older California homes include roof condition, HVAC systems, electrical panels, plumbing, and foundation issues. The decision whether to repair before listing, sell as-is, or offer a price reduction to account for known issues depends on the market, the severity of the issues, and the heirs' financial position and timeline.

Choosing the Right Agent

Not all real estate agents have experience with estate and probate sales. These transactions have documentation requirements, process steps, and legal considerations that differ from standard residential sales. An agent who understands the legal context will coordinate more effectively with the estate attorney and title company, and will avoid missteps that create delays.

When evaluating agents for an inherited property sale, consider asking:

  • Have they previously handled trust sales or probate sales in California?
  • Are they familiar with the documentation required for successor trustees and executors?
  • Do they understand the disclosure requirements specific to estate sales?
  • Do they have experience working alongside estate attorneys and trust officers?

An agent with estate sale experience will also be better positioned to navigate situations involving multiple heirs who may have differing opinions on pricing, timing, and buyer selection. Timeline: Trust Sale vs. Probate Sale

Week 1 to 2

Confirm authority and order title report

Successor trustee assumes role and executes affidavit, or executor obtains Letters from court. Title company ordered. Death certificate obtained.

Week 2 to 4

Title review and property assessment

Preliminary title report reviewed. Liens, encumbrances, and tax status confirmed. Pre-listing inspection ordered. Repair decisions made.

Week 3 to 6

Appraisal and agent selection

Date-of-death appraisal completed for basis purposes. Agent selected. Listing price established based on appraisal and market conditions.

Week 4 to 8

Property listed and under contract

Trust sale: property listed on market, offer accepted, escrow opened. Probate sale with IAEA authority: same process, with 15-day Notice of Proposed Action period before final acceptance.

Week 8 to 12

Close of escrow

Trust sale and IAEA probate sale typically close within 30 to 45 days of offer acceptance. Court confirmation probate sale adds several additional months for the court hearing and overbid process.

Court confirmation adds significant time. A probate sale requiring court confirmation does not follow the standard residential timeline. After the executor accepts an offer, the sale must be noticed to interested parties, a court hearing must be scheduled, and competing overbids are accepted at the hearing. This process typically adds two to four months to the timeline compared to a trust sale or an IAEA-authority sale. Where IAEA authority is available, it is almost always preferable.

When Multiple Heirs Must Agree

Inherited California property frequently involves multiple heirs who must reach agreement on whether to sell, when to sell, and at what price. When heirs are cooperative and aligned, this is straightforward. When they are not, it can derail a sale or extend the timeline significantly.

A few points worth understanding:

Trustees and executors have authority regardless of heir disagreement. A trustee selling trust property under the trust's terms, or an executor with court authority, does not need the beneficiaries' affirmative consent for each decision. Beneficiaries have rights to information and accounting, and they can challenge decisions that breach fiduciary duties, but they cannot simply veto a sale the trustee or executor is authorized to make.

Co-owners of property face different rules. If the property has already transferred to heirs as co-owners, each owner has rights in the property and cannot be forced to sell by the others without a court proceeding. A partition action allows a co-owner to compel a sale through the courts, but this is costly and time-consuming. Heirs who inherit co-ownership should address disagreements early and directly before they escalate to litigation.

Buyouts are a common resolution. When one heir wants to keep the property and others want to sell, a buyout allows the heir who wants to keep it to purchase the others' shares. The mechanics involve agreeing on value, often through appraisal, and either refinancing the property to generate cash for the buyout or using other estate assets.

What Happens at Closing

The closing process for an inherited property sale is similar to a standard California residential sale, with some additional documentation requirements.

At closing, the title company will:

  • Confirm that the trustee, executor, or heir has proper authority to convey title
  • Pay off any outstanding mortgage or liens from sale proceeds
  • Collect and remit any applicable withholding
  • Distribute net proceeds to the estate or trust, or directly to heirs if the property has already distributed
  • Record the deed conveying title to the buyer

California requires withholding of 3.33 percent of the gross sales price from sellers who are non-residents of California, unless an exemption applies. For estate sales, the residency status of the estate or trust, rather than the individual heirs, determines whether withholding is required.

After closing, the executor or trustee is responsible for accounting for the sale proceeds as part of the estate or trust administration. Net proceeds become available for distribution to heirs once the administration is concluded and all claims, debts, and taxes of the estate are satisfied.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who has authority to sell an inherited home in California?

It depends on how the property is held. For trust-owned property, the successor trustee has authority. For probate property, the executor or administrator appointed by the court has authority. Heirs who have received title through distribution can sell as owners.

Do I have to pay capital gains tax when I sell an inherited California home?

Probably not much if you sell shortly after inheriting. The stepped-up basis rule resets your cost basis to fair market value at the date of death. If you sell near that value, the taxable gain is minimal or zero. If the property has appreciated since then, you owe gains on that additional appreciation.

How long does it take to sell an inherited home in California?

A trust sale can often close within 60 to 90 days of the trustee assuming authority. A probate sale with IAEA authority follows a similar timeline. A probate sale requiring court confirmation adds several months for the hearing and overbid process.

Can heirs sell an inherited California home before probate is complete?

Generally no, not on their own authority. The executor or administrator can sell during probate with proper court authority, and the proceeds become an estate asset. Heirs cannot sell independently until title has transferred to them.

Do I need a special real estate agent to sell an inherited home?

Not legally, but an agent with experience in estate and probate sales is practically valuable. These transactions have different documentation requirements and process steps. An agent who understands the legal context coordinates more effectively with the attorney and title company.

What disclosures are required when selling inherited property in California?

California requires disclosure of known material defects. Sellers must disclose what they actually know, even with limited knowledge of the property. The Transfer Disclosure Statement and Seller Property Questionnaire are required. Buyers are entitled to a full inspection period regardless of how the property is sold.
