Trustee Responsibilities for California Real Estate

By William B. Plevy · Attorney | California Real Estate Broker

Being named a trustee, whether for a parent's trust, a sibling's trust, or any other, carries real legal obligations. When the trust holds California real estate, those obligations become particularly tangible. Real property requires active management, insurance, maintenance decisions, and eventually either sale or distribution to beneficiaries. Getting it wrong can expose a trustee to personal liability.

This guide explains the core responsibilities of a trustee dealing with California real estate, from the moment they take on the role through the completion of administration.

The Trustee's Fiduciary Duties

A trustee is a fiduciary, a person who holds a position of trust and is legally required to act in the interest of the beneficiaries, not in their own interest. California law imposes specific duties on trustees, including:

  • Duty of Loyalty: The trustee must administer the trust solely in the interest of the beneficiaries. Personal benefit to the trustee at the expense of the trust is prohibited.
  • Duty of Prudence: The trustee must exercise the care of a prudent person in administering the trust, including in managing, selling, or distributing trust assets like real property.
  • Duty to Inform and Account: Trustees must keep beneficiaries reasonably informed about the trust and its administration, and must account for trust transactions when required.
  • Duty of Impartiality: When there are multiple beneficiaries with different interests (for example, a current income beneficiary and a remainder beneficiary), the trustee must act impartially in managing the trust property.
  • Duty to Avoid Conflicts of Interest: Trustees must not put themselves in situations where their personal interests conflict with their duties to the beneficiaries.

These duties apply with full force when the trust holds real property. A trustee who makes decisions about real estate without considering these obligations may face personal liability to beneficiaries.

Securing and Protecting the Property

One of the trustee's first responsibilities when a settlor dies and real property is involved is to physically secure and protect the property. This is especially important when:

  • The property is or will become vacant
  • The property has personal property inside that needs to be inventoried or protected
  • The property is in a location that requires active maintenance (lawn care, snow removal, etc.)
  • The property is in an area with elevated vandalism or theft risk

Practical steps include: changing the locks if necessary, posting no trespassing notices for vacant properties, arranging for regular inspections, and ensuring mail is collected or forwarded so that the property does not appear abandoned.

Insurance and Occupancy

Maintaining adequate insurance coverage on trust real estate is a fiduciary obligation. Several insurance issues commonly arise in trust administration:

Vacancy exclusions. Standard homeowner's insurance policies often exclude or limit coverage once a property has been vacant for 30 to 60 days. Trustees who allow a property to become vacant without updating the insurance coverage may find that losses are not covered. A vacant property endorsement or separate vacant property policy may be needed.

Notifying the insurer. The insurer should be notified of the policyholder's death and the property's occupancy status. The trustee should request confirmation of current coverage levels.

Occupancy by a beneficiary. If a beneficiary is living in the property, the trustee should address: who is responsible for utilities and maintenance, whether rent is expected, and how this arrangement interacts with the trust's administrative timeline.

Liability insurance. The property should carry adequate liability insurance to protect against slip-and-fall and other premises liability claims, especially if the property has visitors, tenants, or a pool.

Communicating with Beneficiaries

Beneficiaries of a trust have legal rights to information about the administration of the trust, including decisions about real property. California law requires trustees to keep beneficiaries reasonably informed and to provide specific notices at certain points in the administration.

From a practical standpoint, good communication serves the trustee as much as it serves the beneficiaries. Beneficiaries who feel informed and included are far less likely to object to trustee decisions or file claims. Beneficiaries who feel excluded or ignored are far more likely to escalate.

Best practices for beneficiary communication about real property:

  • Inform beneficiaries early about the status of the property and the anticipated timeline
  • Provide a written accounting of property-related expenses
  • Discuss major decisions, such as whether to sell, make significant repairs, or rent the property, before acting
  • Document all communications and keep copies
  • If beneficiaries disagree, seek legal counsel before proceeding

Property Valuation

Getting an accurate valuation of trust real property is important for multiple reasons: estate tax filings, accounting to beneficiaries, sale pricing, equalization among beneficiaries, and insurance purposes.

A formal appraisal by a licensed California real estate appraiser is typically the appropriate method for establishing property value in a trust administration. While a broker's opinion of value or a comparative market analysis from a real estate agent can be helpful for sale pricing purposes, a formal appraisal is typically needed for estate tax purposes and may be needed for other legal purposes.

Trustees should obtain valuation at or near the date of death (for estate purposes) and again closer to a sale (for pricing purposes). Properties can change significantly in value during a long administration.

Managing the Sale Process

When a trustee sells trust real property in California, several steps are involved:

  • Establish authority. The trustee must be able to demonstrate legal authority to sell. This typically means having a Certification of Trust and the trust document available, and confirming that the trust authorizes the trustee to sell real property.
  • Prepare the property. Trustees should address condition issues that will affect the sale price or the ability to close. This involves balancing the cost of repairs against the expected improvement in value.
  • Engage a licensed real estate broker. Trustees should work with a qualified California real estate broker to list and sell the property. The trustee has a fiduciary duty to obtain a fair price for the property, which typically means exposing it to the market rather than selling off-market to a connected buyer.
  • Review and negotiate offers. Offers should be evaluated carefully. The trustee's obligation is to the beneficiaries, not to simply accept the first offer or the highest offer without due diligence.
  • Provide required disclosures. California law requires specific disclosures in real estate transactions, including trustee sales. See the disclosures section below.
  • Close the transaction. Escrow and closing proceed similarly to any other California real estate sale, with the trustee signing closing documents in their fiduciary capacity.
  • Distribute or invest the proceeds. Sale proceeds must be handled in accordance with the trust document and the trustee's fiduciary obligations.

Repairs, Improvements, and Maintenance

Trustees are generally obligated to maintain trust real property in reasonable condition. However, making major improvements beyond routine maintenance raises questions about authority, cost, and whether the improvements serve the beneficiaries' collective interests.

Consider the following framework:

  • Emergency repairs: Must generally be made promptly to prevent further damage or liability. Document the basis for emergency status.
  • Routine maintenance: Ongoing maintenance to preserve the property's condition is typically within a trustee's authority and duty.
  • Pre-sale repairs: If the property is to be sold, the trustee should analyze the return on repair investment. Spending $20,000 on repairs that increase the sale price by $30,000 may be prudent; spending $20,000 on upgrades that add no value may not be.
  • Major improvements: Trustees should generally obtain beneficiary consent or legal advice before making major capital improvements to trust property.

All repair and maintenance expenditures should be documented with invoices, before-and-after photos, and contractor information. This documentation protects the trustee in the event of a beneficiary dispute about how trust funds were spent.

Disclosures in a Trustee Sale

California real estate law imposes significant disclosure obligations on sellers, including trustee sellers. The principal disclosure tools include:

Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS): California requires a TDS in most residential real estate transactions. There is a specific version for trust, estate, and conservatorship sales. Even if the trustee has not lived in the property, the trustee must disclose known material defects and conditions to the buyer.

Natural Hazard Disclosure (NHD): Sellers in California must provide an NHD report disclosing whether the property is in certain natural hazard zones (flood, fire, earthquake, etc.).

Death disclosure: California law requires disclosure if a death occurred in the property within three years prior to the sale, with limited exceptions for certain causes of death.

Environmental hazards: Known environmental hazards (lead paint, asbestos, underground storage tanks, etc.) must be disclosed.

Trustees should not take a narrow view of their disclosure obligations. Disclosing known issues protects both the buyer and the trustee. Failing to disclose can result in post-closing claims that are more costly to defend than the original issue was to address.

Selecting a Real Estate Broker

Choosing the right real estate broker is an important decision in a trustee sale. A trustee who selects a broker based on personal relationships rather than competence may face questions about the quality of that decision. Consider:

  • Experience with probate, trust, and estate sales in California
  • Familiarity with the local market where the property is located
  • Track record and references
  • Marketing plan and approach to pricing
  • Understanding of the trustee's fiduciary obligations and the disclosure requirements

Trustees should interview multiple agents if possible and document the selection process. This protects the trustee from later claims that the broker selection was self-serving or incompetent.

Recordkeeping and Documentation

Trustees administering real property should maintain thorough records throughout the process. Good recordkeeping serves several purposes:

  • Enables accurate accounting to beneficiaries
  • Provides a defense if beneficiaries later challenge trustee decisions
  • Supports estate tax returns and other filings
  • Ensures compliance with legal requirements

Key documents to retain include: the trust document, correspondence with beneficiaries, insurance policies, property tax records, all repair and maintenance invoices, the appraisal, the listing agreement, the purchase contract, escrow closing documents, and a final accounting of sale proceeds.

Avoiding and Managing Beneficiary Disputes

Beneficiary disputes over trust real property are common and can be expensive and emotionally draining. Proactive communication and transparent administration are the best preventive tools.

When disputes arise despite best efforts, trustees should:

  • Consult with legal counsel before taking any action that a beneficiary has formally objected to
  • Consider mediation as an alternative to litigation
  • Document all interactions with objecting beneficiaries
  • Not allow a dispute over one issue to paralyze the entire administration

In some cases, a trustee who faces irreconcilable beneficiary conflict may petition the court for instructions on how to proceed. This is a legitimate tool that protects both the trustee and the administration.

Common Trustee Mistakes

  • Allowing the property insurance to lapse after the settlor's death
  • Delaying action on a vacant or deteriorating property
  • Making major decisions without consulting beneficiaries or legal counsel
  • Allowing a beneficiary to live in the property rent-free without a written agreement
  • Failing to obtain a formal appraisal
  • Selling to a related party without full transparency and beneficiary consent
  • Failing to document repair decisions and expenditures
  • Missing required disclosures in a trustee sale

Trustee Real Estate Checklist

  • Locate and review the trust document and confirm trustee authority
  • Obtain Certification of Trust from legal counsel
  • Secure the property and change locks if needed
  • Review and update homeowner's insurance; address vacancy issues
  • Continue mortgage and property tax payments
  • Notify beneficiaries of the property's status and your role
  • Obtain a formal property appraisal
  • Consult with legal and tax advisors about Proposition 19 and distribution options
  • Decide on sale vs. distribution in consultation with beneficiaries and advisors
  • If selling: engage a qualified broker, prepare disclosures, list and market properly
  • Document all decisions, expenditures, and communications
  • Provide beneficiaries with a final accounting of trust real estate proceeds

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a trustee sell trust property without beneficiary approval?

Generally, yes, if the trust document gives the trustee authority to sell, which most do. However, the trustee must act prudently and in the beneficiaries' best interests, and should communicate with beneficiaries before selling. If a trustee sells property in a way that harms beneficiaries, they may face personal liability.

Does a trustee have to disclose defects when selling trust property?

Yes. California law requires disclosure of known material defects even in trust and estate sales. A trustee cannot turn a blind eye to known problems. The Trust Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS) and related disclosures are required in most trustee residential sales.

Can a trustee buy the trust property themselves?

Self-dealing, meaning a trustee purchasing trust property for themselves, is generally prohibited or requires specific authorization. In California, a trustee who wants to buy trust property must typically obtain court approval or explicit written consent from all beneficiaries after full disclosure.

What if a beneficiary objects to the sale?

A beneficiary who objects to a trustee's decision to sell may make their objection known, but a trustee with proper authority is not necessarily bound by that objection. However, trustees facing beneficiary objections should consult with legal counsel before proceeding to ensure they are following proper procedures and documenting their decision-making.

How is a trustee sale different from a regular real estate sale?

The trustee signs documents in a fiduciary capacity (for example, "Jane Smith, as Trustee of the Smith Family Trust"). The seller is the trust, not an individual. There are specific disclosure forms for trust sales. The trustee owes fiduciary duties to the beneficiaries in addition to normal seller obligations to the buyer.

Can the trust property be rented while the administration is pending?

In many cases, yes, trustees can rent trust property while administration is ongoing. However, doing so creates ongoing landlord obligations, including compliance with California landlord-tenant law. The trustee should also consider the impact of rental on eventual sale (tenants have rights that affect the sale process and timing).
