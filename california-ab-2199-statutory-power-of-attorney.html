California Just Updated Its Statutory Power of Attorney: What Changed Under AB 2199

By William Plevy · Attorney, admitted in California, New York, and D.C. · Published July 2026
Legislative status: AB 2199 was signed by Governor Newsom on June 30, 2026. The revised statutory form is not immediately operative. Practitioners should monitor the operative date, expected in 2027, and update templates and client materials in advance.

California's statutory power of attorney form has long been a workable baseline, and also a document that experienced estate planners frequently bypass in favor of custom-drafted instruments. AB 2199, authored by Assemblymember Alexandra Macedo and signed by Governor Newsom on June 30, 2026, makes the most meaningful revisions to that form in years. Three additions in particular: successor agent appointment, conservator nomination, and express digital asset authority. All three address gaps that have generated real-world problems in practice.

This article is written for practitioners: estate planning attorneys, fiduciaries, and others who work with powers of attorney as part of their professional practice. It assumes familiarity with the Uniform Statutory Form Power of Attorney Act and California's Probate Code framework. The goal is a clear account of what changed, why the changes matter for drafting, and what to do before the new form becomes operative.

Background: The Statutory Form and Its Limitations

California's statutory power of attorney form is codified in the Uniform Statutory Form Power of Attorney Act, Probate Code sections 4400 through 4465. The form provides a standardized template that, when properly executed, creates a durable power of attorney granting the agent authority to act in the principal's financial affairs. Its simplicity is both its strength and its limitation.

For straightforward situations where a principal wants to designate a single trusted agent with broad authority, the statutory form has served reasonably well. But practitioners have long navigated around several structural weaknesses, including the absence of a clear mechanism for successor agents, no conservator nomination provision, and no express digital asset language. These gaps have not been fatal; custom drafting addresses all of them, but they have meant that a principal who executed only the statutory form was left with a document that could fail at the moments it was needed most.

AB 2199 repeal and replaces Probate Code Section 4401 and adds new Section 4466. The structural approach is conservative: it revises the statutory form rather than overhauling the underlying Act. That is the right call. The Act's framework is sound. What the form needed was catch-up with drafting practices that have evolved considerably since digital assets became a central feature of most principals' financial lives, and since the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act gave agents a statutory hook for digital access that the POA form itself had not yet incorporated.

The Three Substantive Changes

AB 2199 makes three principal additions to the statutory form. They are distinct in purpose but share a common theme: reducing the circumstances under which a validly executed power of attorney fails to function as the principal intended.

Successor Agents and Co-Agents

The original statutory form allowed designation of a single agent. If that agent was unable or unwilling to serve (due to death, incapacity, resignation, or changed circumstances), the POA failed. The principal would need a new document, assuming they still had capacity to execute one. If they did not, the family was left with a court proceeding.

This failure mode was well understood by practitioners, who addressed it in custom drafting by naming successor agents in a priority order and specifying the conditions under which succession occurred. The statutory form offered no equivalent mechanism.

AB 2199 corrects this by allowing the principal to appoint a successor agent or co-agents within the statutory form itself. The practical effect is significant. A principal who uses the statutory form can now build in the same redundancy that custom-drafted documents have long provided, without requiring the principal to seek out an attorney who drafts around the form's limitations. For attorneys using the statutory form as a starting point, the new provision aligns the form with standard practice.

Co-agent appointment raises its own drafting considerations that practitioners should think through with clients. When co-agents must act jointly, the instrument gains a check-and-balance structure but loses the operational flexibility that a single-agent POA provides. When co-agents may act independently, operational flexibility is preserved but the risk of inconsistent or conflicting actions increases. The new form enables the choice; it does not make it.

Drafting note. For clients who name co-agents, the instrument should specify whether co-agents must act jointly or may act independently, and should address what happens when co-agents disagree. Leaving these questions to implication creates the kind of ambiguity that invites litigation.

Conservator Nomination

The second addition allows the principal to nominate their designated agent to serve as conservator of the estate if a court ever needs to appoint one. This is a significant addition that connects the POA to the conservatorship framework in a way the original form did not.

The existing conservatorship statute, Probate Code Section 1810, already allows a principal to nominate a conservator in a separate written instrument, and that nomination carries presumptive weight with the court. What AB 2199 does is bring that nomination capability into the statutory POA form itself, so a principal who wants their chosen agent to also serve as conservator can accomplish both in a single document without a separate nomination instrument.

This matters most in the gap cases: situations where the principal has executed a power of attorney but the agent's authority under that document is being challenged, or where the agent's actions under the POA are insufficient to manage the principal's affairs and a court proceeding becomes necessary. In those situations, having the conservator nomination within the four corners of the POA strengthens the agent's position before the court and reduces the family's exposure to a contested conservatorship.

Practitioners should note that a conservator nomination is not a guarantee of appointment. The court retains authority to appoint a different person if the nominated conservator is not suitable or if appointment would not be in the principal's best interest. But the nomination shifts the burden and carries substantial practical weight.

Probate Code Section 4466 (new)

AB 2199 adds Section 4466, which provides the mechanism for incorporating the conservator nomination and successor agent provisions into the statutory form. Practitioners should review the full statutory text before updating templates.

Digital Asset Authority

The third change is the one with the broadest practical reach. AB 2199 adds express authority over digital assets and electronic communications to the statutory form, tied to California's Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act.

The Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act, enacted in California as Probate Code sections 870 through 884, governs an agent's ability to access a principal's digital assets. Digital assets include the content of email accounts, social media, cloud storage, cryptocurrency, online banking interfaces, and other electronic records. The Act establishes a priority hierarchy: a platform's online tool takes precedence, followed by specific directions in a legal document, followed by terms-of-service agreements.

Before AB 2199, an agent relying on the statutory POA form had no express digital asset authority within the document itself. An agent attempting to access a principal's email or financial accounts through an online platform would need to rely on general authority provisions or a separate instrument. Many platforms were unwilling to cooperate without explicit authorization language, and some declined outright regardless of the agent's general authority under the POA.

The new form addresses this by expressly incorporating digital asset authority under the Act. This means an agent acting under the updated statutory form has clear textual authority over the principal's digital assets, including email and electronic communications, without requiring a separate document or custom drafting language.

For estate planning attorneys, this is the change with the most immediate implications for existing practice. Many custom-drafted POAs already include digital asset provisions, but the language varies significantly in quality and specificity. The AB 2199 revision creates an opportunity to audit existing form language against the Act and ensure that what practitioners are drafting actually accomplishes what their clients expect.

The Three-Tier Hierarchy and Why It Still Matters

Even with express POA authority, the Act's three-tier hierarchy means the POA is not automatically sufficient to compel a platform to provide access. If the platform offers an online legacy tool or account-successor designation mechanism (Google, Apple, and Facebook all offer these), that designation takes priority over the POA. A principal who has designated someone other than their agent as the account successor has, in effect, overridden the POA for that platform.

Practitioners advising clients on digital assets should address the platform-tool tier as part of the planning conversation, not leave it to the agent to discover at incapacity. The POA provision added by AB 2199 is necessary but not always sufficient.

Effect on Existing Documents

AB 2199 revises the statutory form. It does not retroactively affect the validity of powers of attorney that were executed before the new form becomes operative. A durable power of attorney executed under the prior statutory form, or under a custom-drafted instrument, remains valid to the extent it was validly executed and has not been revoked.

The practical question for practitioners is not whether existing documents are valid, but whether they are optimal. A client who executed a statutory-form POA five years ago has a document that lacks successor agent provisions, lacks a conservator nomination, and lacks express digital asset authority. None of those absences invalidates the document. But if the named agent predeceases the principal, or if the principal's financial life now includes significant digital assets, the document may not function as intended.

AB 2199 is a reasonable prompt for an audit of the existing documents in a practitioner's client base, particularly for clients who executed statutory-form POAs without supplemental custom drafting. That audit need not be urgent, since the new form is not yet operative, but it should be on the planning horizon before 2027.

Practice Notes for Drafting Attorneys

Several specific items are worth flagging for practitioners who will be updating templates or client questionnaires in advance of the new form's operative date.

Successor agent priority and conditions of succession. The new form allows successor agent designation, but the drafting choices around succession conditions matter. A successor who takes over automatically upon the primary agent's death is different from one who takes over only after a formal determination of incapacity or resignation. Clients should understand the difference and make an affirmative choice.

Co-agent decision rules. As noted above, co-agent designation without clear decision rules is an invitation to ambiguity. If the client's goal is redundancy rather than shared oversight, a successor agent structure is usually cleaner than a co-agent structure.

Conservator nomination scope. The nomination provision addresses conservator of the estate. Clients who want the same person to serve as conservator of the person should ensure that nomination is made separately or that the document addresses both, depending on how the final statutory text is structured.

Digital asset inventory and platform-tool tier. Before the POA becomes operative, clients should conduct a digital asset inventory and, where platforms offer online legacy tools, make designations consistent with their estate plan. A POA that names one person as agent and a platform tool that names another creates a conflict the POA cannot win for that platform.

Template review timing. The new form is not yet operative. Updating client-facing templates before the operative date creates a risk of using a form that does not yet match the statute. The better practice is to flag the changes now, prepare updated templates, and implement on or after the operative date. Client questionnaires can be updated earlier to begin capturing successor agent preferences and digital asset information as part of intake.

Clients with existing statutory-form POAs. For clients who executed only the statutory form without supplemental drafting, a review conversation is warranted before 2027. The questions are straightforward: Is the named agent still the right person? Is there a successor if not? Are there significant digital assets? Has the client made platform-tool designations? If the answers suggest gaps, updating the document while the client has capacity is preferable to discovering those gaps at incapacity.

Frequently Asked Questions

When does AB 2199 take effect?

AB 2199 was signed by Governor Newsom on June 30, 2026. The revised statutory form is not immediately operative. Practitioners should monitor the operative date, expected in 2027, and update templates and client materials in advance of that date rather than immediately.

Does AB 2199 invalidate existing powers of attorney?

No. Existing durable powers of attorney that were validly executed remain effective. AB 2199 revises the statutory form, not the underlying validity requirements for POAs generally. The bill is a reason to review existing documents for functional gaps, not a reason to re-execute them as a matter of legal necessity.

What are the three main changes AB 2199 makes to the statutory POA form?

First, it allows the principal to appoint a successor agent or co-agents, so the POA does not fail if the first-choice agent cannot serve. Second, it allows the principal to nominate their agent to also serve as conservator of the estate if a court ever needs to appoint one. Third, it adds express authority over digital assets and electronic communications under California's Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act.

How does the digital asset provision interact with platform-level account tools?

Under California's Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act, a platform's own online legacy or account-successor tool takes priority over a power of attorney. The AB 2199 digital asset provision gives agents clear textual authority within the POA, but it does not override a conflicting platform-tool designation. Comprehensive digital asset planning requires addressing both the POA and the platform-tool tier.
